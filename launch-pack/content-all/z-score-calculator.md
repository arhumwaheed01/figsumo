H1: Z-Score Calculator
A z-score tells you how many standard deviations a value sits above or below the mean. It puts results from different scales on a common footing. Is 78 on a hard test better than 88 on an easy one? Enter the value, the mean and the standard deviation, and this calculator gives the z-score.
How to use this calculator
1. Value (x): the observation you're interested in.
2. Mean (μ): the average of the group.
3. Standard deviation (σ): the spread of the group; must be positive.
Result: the z-score, to six decimal places.
The formula
z = (x − μ) ÷ σ
z > 0: above the mean; z < 0: below; z = 0: exactly average.
To go the other way: x = μ + z × σ.
Worked examples
Example 1: a test score
You scored 85 on a test with mean 70 and SD 10: z = (85 − 70) ÷ 10 = 1.5. On a normal curve, that's higher than about 93.3% of scores (Φ(1.5) = 0.9332).
Example 2: comparing two tests
Test A: 78, mean 65, SD 8 → z = 1.625
Test B: 88, mean 80, SD 5 → z = 1.600
The 78 is the stronger result relative to the class, despite the lower raw score.
Example 3: below average
Height 160 cm where adults average 170 cm with SD 7 cm: z = -1.428571, about the 7.7th percentile.
Example 4: finding a cutoff
For the top 5% on the test in Example 1 (z ≈ 1.645), you'd need 70 + 1.645 × 10 = 86.45.

Where z-scores are used
Standardised tests: many scale scores are transformed z-scores.
Growth charts: child height and weight are often reported as z-scores against reference data.
Quality control: values beyond ±3 are treated as unusual.
Hypothesis tests: the z-test statistic is a z-score for a sample mean.
Outlier screening: |z| above 3 often flags a value for checking.
Understanding your results
The percentile rule assumes normality. For skewed data, z still measures distance in SDs, but the percentile table won't be accurate.
Population vs sample: use the population mean and SD where known; otherwise sample estimates.
Units cancel, so z has no units.
Tips and common mistakes
Using variance instead of SD. If you're given σ² = 100, enter σ = 10.
Subtracting the wrong way round. It's x minus μ.
Reading z as a percentage. A z of 1.5 isn't 1.5%.
Applying the normal table to small or skewed samples.
Notes for US, Canada and UK readers
IQ tests in all three countries are usually scaled to a mean of 100 and a standard deviation of 15, so a score of 130 is z = (130 − 100) ÷ 15 = 2.0, about the 97.7th percentile. US standardized tests, Canadian provincial exams and UK school assessments may report scaled scores or percentiles rather than z-scores, so check how the publisher defines its scale before converting.
Frequently asked questions
What is a z-score?
The number of standard deviations a value is from the mean.
Can a z-score be negative?
Yes; it means the value is below the mean.
What is a good z-score?
It depends on context. Above 2 is unusually high for normal data.
How do I convert a z-score to a percentile?
Use the standard normal table (Φ).
What's the z-score for 95% confidence?
±1.96 for two-sided intervals.
What if the standard deviation is zero?
z is undefined; every value equals the mean.
