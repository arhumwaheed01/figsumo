H1: Annuity Calculator
This calculator shows what a series of equal, regular deposits grows to by the end of a set number of years at a steady annual return. That is the "accumulation" phase of an annuity, and the same arithmetic applies to any plan where you pay in a fixed amount each month, quarter or year. Enter the deposit, the return, the number of years and how many deposits you make per year.
How to use this calculator
1. Periodic payment: the amount you pay in each period, before any fees. For a deferred annuity contract this is your scheduled premium; for a savings plan it is your standing order.
2. Annual return: the yearly rate you expect the money to earn, as a percentage. Use a net figure (after the contract's annual charges) if you know it, because fees reduce the rate you actually earn.
3. Years: how long you keep paying in.
4. Payments per year: 12 for monthly, 4 for quarterly, 2 for half-yearly, 1 for annual.
The results show the accumulated value at the end of the final period and the total payments made. The difference between the two is the growth earned.
The formula
The tool uses the future value of an ordinary annuity, which assumes each payment is made at the end of its period:
FV = PMT × ((1 + i)^n − 1) / i
FV: accumulated value at the end
PMT: the periodic payment
i: interest per period = annual return ÷ payments per year
n: number of payments = years × payments per year
Interest compounds once per payment period, so a monthly plan compounds monthly and a quarterly plan compounds quarterly. If the return is 0%, the result is simply PMT × n.
If you pay at the start of each period instead (an "annuity due"), every payment earns one extra period of interest, so the result is FV × (1 + i). The calculator does not have a timing switch at present; multiply by (1 + i) yourself to see the difference.
Worked examples
Example 1: $500 a month for 20 years at 5%
i = 0.05 ÷ 12 = 0.0041667; n = 20 × 12 = 240
FV = 500 × ((1.0041667)^240 − 1) ÷ 0.0041667 = $205,516.83
Total paid in: 500 × 240 = $120,000.00
Growth: $85,516.83
With payments at the start of each month instead, the value would be $206,373.15.
The return assumption matters more than most people expect. The same deposits give $183,387.31 at 4% and $231,020.45 at 6%.
Example 2: $1,500 a quarter for 15 years at 6%
i = 0.06 ÷ 4 = 0.015; n = 15 × 4 = 60
FV = 1,500 × ((1.015)^60 − 1) ÷ 0.015 = $144,321.98
Total paid in: $90,000.00; growth: $54,321.98
Paying the same $6,000 a year as $500 a month (12 payments a year) gives $145,409.36, slightly more, because money paid monthly starts earning sooner and compounds more often.
Understanding your results
Accumulated value is a projection, not a guarantee. It assumes the same return every period. Real returns vary year to year, and with a variable annuity they can be negative.
Total payments made is your own money. Growth is everything above it.
No tax or inflation is applied. In 20 years, prices will probably be higher, so the buying power of the final sum will be lower than its face value. Use the inflation calculator to restate it in today's money.
No contract charges are deducted unless you lower the return to allow for them. Annual fees of 1–2% are common in some annuity products (check your contract's fee table), and over 20 years they make a large difference.
Types of annuity and what this tool models
An annuity contract usually has two phases. In the accumulation phase you pay in, as this calculator models. In the payout (annuitization) phase the insurer converts the balance into income; to model that, use the annuity payout calculator.
Fixed annuity: the insurer credits a stated rate for a period. Your input rate is close to reality for that period.
Variable annuity: the money is invested in sub-accounts, so the return is not known in advance. Treat any rate you enter as an assumption and try a range.
Indexed annuity: credits are linked to a market index with caps or participation rates. The yearly credit can be zero, so a flat input rate is only a rough guide.
Immediate vs deferred: an immediate annuity starts paying out soon after a single premium and has no accumulation phase. This tool is for deferred, regular-premium plans.
Surrender charges often apply if you withdraw money in the first years of a contract. The calculator does not include them.
Country notes
United States: gains inside a deferred annuity are tax-deferred, and withdrawals of earnings before age 59½ can trigger a 10% additional tax as well as ordinary income tax (IRS Publication 575). The SEC and FINRA publish investor guidance on variable and indexed annuities.
Canada: annuities are sold by life insurers. Registered annuities bought with RRSP or RRIF money are taxed as income when paid. Contributions to your plan may be limited by your RRSP room.
United Kingdom: "annuity" usually means the income product bought with a pension pot at retirement. Regular savings into a pension or ISA follow the same growth formula, so this tool still works for those. See the ISA calculator for the annual allowance.
Tips and common mistakes
Using the gross rate when fees are charged. Subtract the annual charge from the expected return.
Mixing up payments per year and years. "Payments per year" is a count (12, 4, 1), not an amount.
Assuming a variable product earns a fixed rate. Run the calculation at a low, middle and high rate to see a range.
Ignoring the payout phase. A large accumulated value does not tell you the income it buys. Annuity payout rates depend on age, interest rates and options such as guarantees.
Frequently asked questions
Does the calculator assume payments at the start or end of each period?
At the end. This is the standard ordinary-annuity convention. If you pay at the start, multiply the result by (1 + annual rate ÷ payments per year).
How often does interest compound?
Once per payment period. A monthly plan compounds monthly; an annual plan compounds annually.
Can I include money I already have in the annuity?
Not directly. Work out the growth of the existing balance separately with the future value calculator and add the two results.
What return should I enter?
For a fixed annuity, the guaranteed rate in the contract. For variable or indexed products there is no single answer, so try a cautious and an optimistic rate and plan around the cautious one.
Is the accumulated value what I can take out?
Not necessarily. Surrender charges, market value adjustments and taxes can reduce what you receive, especially in the early years of a contract.
Why does monthly beat annual for the same yearly total?
Each monthly payment starts earning interest earlier in the year, and monthly compounding adds interest on interest more often.
Does this tell me my retirement income?
No. It gives the balance at the end of saving. Use the annuity payout or retirement calculator to turn a balance into income.
