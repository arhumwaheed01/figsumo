H1: ROI Calculator
Return on investment (ROI) measures how much you gained or lost relative to what you put in. It's the quickest way to compare the results of different investments, business projects or purchases. Enter the net gain and the cost to get the ROI as a percentage and the final value.
How to use this calculator
1. Amount gained (net profit): what you made after costs. Enter a negative number for a loss. Include income received (dividends, rent) and subtract fees, commissions and taxes you want to account for.
2. Cost of investment: the total you invested, including purchase fees.
Results: ROI (%) and final value (cost plus gain).
The formula
ROI = Net gain ÷ Cost × 100
Net gain = Final value + Income received − Cost
ROI doesn't consider time. A 15% return over one year and over ten years show the same ROI, which is why you should also look at the annualized figure:
Annualized ROI = (1 + ROI)^(1 ÷ years) − 1
Worked examples
Example 1: the default figures
Gain $15,000 on a $100,000 investment
ROI = 15,000 ÷ 100,000 = 15%; final value $115,000.00
If that took 3 years, the annualized return is 4.77% a year.
Example 2: comparing two investments
A: invest $10,000, gain $4,000 over 2 years → ROI 40%, annualized 18.32%
B: invest $20,000, gain $9,000 over 5 years → ROI 45%, annualized 7.71%
B has the higher ROI, but A earned more per year. For investments of different lengths, compare annualized figures.
Example 3: counting fees and a loss
Sell for $12,000 a holding bought for $10,000, with $150 in purchase fees and $90 in sale fees.
Net gain = 12,000 − 10,000 − 150 − 90 = $1,760.00; cost including purchase fees $10,150
ROI = 17.34%
A loss works the same way: losing $2,500 on $10,000 is an ROI of −25%. To get back to $10,000 from $7,500 you then need a gain of 33.33%, more than the percentage lost.
Example 4: a rental property bought with cash
Purchase $250,000 plus $10,000 of closing costs: cost $260,000
Net rent after expenses: $14,400 a year for 5 years = $72,000
Sold for $300,000, less $18,000 in selling costs = $282,000
Net gain = 282,000 + 72,000 − 260,000 = $94,000
ROI = 36.15%, annualized about 6.37% a year
The annualized figure here treats the gain as if it all arrived at the end. Because the rent came in every year, the true annual return is a little higher; the IRR calculator handles that timing.
Example 5: an advertising campaign
You spend $2,000 on ads that bring $9,000 of sales at a 40% gross margin:
Gross profit from those sales: $3,600
ROI = (3,600 − 2,000) ÷ 2,000 = 80%
Return on ad spend (ROAS) = 9,000 ÷ 2,000 = 4.5×
ROAS uses revenue, so it always looks better than ROI. A ROAS of 2.5× at a 40% margin is break-even: 2.5 × 0.40 = 1.
Real ROI after inflation
The 15% ROI over 3 years in Example 1, with 3% inflation a year, is a real return of 1.15 ÷ 1.03³ − 1 = 5.24% over the period.
Understanding your results
ROI is a ratio, so it works for anything with a cost and a gain: shares, property, a marketing campaign or new equipment.
It ignores timing of cash flows. If money goes in or comes out at different times, use the IRR calculator or the average return calculator.
It ignores risk. A higher ROI from a riskier bet isn't automatically better.
Inflation: a 15% ROI over 3 years with 3% annual inflation is a smaller real gain; see the inflation calculator.
ROI in business decisions
For a project, net gain is the extra profit it generates (revenue minus extra costs). A $5,000 marketing campaign that brings in $8,000 of extra gross profit has an ROI of 60%. Make sure "gain" is profit, not revenue, and include staff time. For how long it takes to recover the outlay, see the payback period calculator.
Country notes
ROI is calculated the same way everywhere, but after-tax ROI depends on the country:
United States: gains on assets held over a year qualify for long-term capital gains rates; see the capital gains tax calculator.
Canada: half of a capital gain is taxable for most individuals [VERIFY]; gains inside a TFSA are tax-free.
United Kingdom: Capital Gains Tax applies above the annual exempt amount (£3,000 for 2026/27 [VERIFY]); ISAs shelter gains.

Tips and common mistakes
Using revenue as gain. Subtract the costs.
Leaving out fees and taxes. They can turn a small gain into a loss.
Comparing ROIs over different periods without annualizing.
Forgetting income. Dividends, interest and rent count toward the gain.
Frequently asked questions
How do I calculate ROI?
Divide the net gain by the cost and multiply by 100.
What is a good ROI?
It depends on the risk and time involved. Compare it with alternatives of similar risk, such as an index fund or a savings rate.
Can ROI be negative?
Yes. A negative ROI means you lost money.
What's the difference between ROI and annualized return?
ROI is the total return over the whole period; annualized return spreads it into an equivalent yearly rate.
Does ROI include taxes?
Only if you subtract them from the gain. Doing so gives after-tax ROI.
What's the difference between ROI and IRR?
ROI ignores timing; IRR accounts for when each cash flow happens.
