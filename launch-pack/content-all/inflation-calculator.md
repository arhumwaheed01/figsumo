H1: Inflation Calculator
Inflation is the rise in prices over time, which means each dollar, pound or loonie buys a little less each year. This calculator shows the effect at a steady inflation rate in both directions: what something that costs a given amount today would cost in the future, and what a future amount is worth in today's money.
How to use this calculator
1. Amount today: a price, salary, savings balance or budget in today's money.
2. Years: how far ahead you want to look.
3. Inflation rate: the yearly rate you expect, as a percentage. Central banks in the US, Canada and the UK each aim for 2% inflation over time, which is a common starting assumption for long-range planning. For the recent past, use the official consumer price index (CPI) figures listed in the sources.
Results:
Future cost (same purchasing power): what you'd need in the future to buy what the amount buys today.
Today's dollars equivalent: what the same nominal amount, received in the future, is worth in today's money.
The formula
Future cost       = Amount × (1 + i)^n
Today's equivalent = Amount ÷ (1 + i)^n
i: inflation rate as a decimal
n: number of years
Inflation compounds: each year's rise applies to the already higher price level.
Worked examples
Example 1: $1,000 over 10 years at 3%
Future cost = 1,000 × 1.03^10 = $1,343.92
Today's equivalent of $1,000 received in 10 years = 1,000 ÷ 1.03^10 = $744.09
At 3% a year, prices roughly double in about 23.4 years (the rule of 72 gives 72 ÷ 3 = 24).
Example 2: a $65,000 salary over 20 years at 2.5%
To keep the same buying power, the salary would need to be $106,510.07 in 20 years.
A $65,000 salary that never rose would be worth only $39,667.61 in today's money by then.
Understanding your results
Future cost is useful for setting savings targets: retirement income, college, a house deposit.
Today's equivalent shows the real value of a fixed future amount, such as a fixed pension or a sum promised years from now.
Inflation is not constant. Actual rates swing, sometimes sharply. Try a few rates to see a range.
Personal inflation differs. Official CPI tracks an average basket. Your costs may rise faster or slower depending on what you spend on (rent, fuel, childcare, healthcare).
Inflation and your money
Real return: an investment earning 5% when inflation is 3% has a real return of (1.05 ÷ 1.03) − 1 = 1.94%.
Pay rises: a 2% raise when inflation is 4% is a real pay cut of −1.92%.
Cash savings: money in an account paying less than inflation loses buying power, even though the balance grows.
Debt: fixed-rate debt gets easier to repay in real terms when inflation is high, because your income tends to rise while the payment stays the same.
Working backwards: the average rate between two prices
If a basket cost $100 twelve years ago and costs $150 today, the average annual inflation rate is (150 ÷ 100)^(1 ÷ 12) − 1 = 3.44%. This calculator doesn't solve for the rate yet, but the formula works with any two prices and a number of years.
Country notes
United States: the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the Consumer Price Index (CPI-U) monthly, and its CPI Inflation Calculator converts prices between past years. The Federal Reserve's 2% target is defined using the PCE price index.
Canada: Statistics Canada publishes the CPI monthly, and the Bank of Canada's inflation-control target is the 2% midpoint of a 1–3% range. The Bank of Canada also offers a historical inflation calculator.
United Kingdom: the Office for National Statistics publishes CPI, CPIH and RPI. The Bank of England's target is 2% CPI inflation. Some pensions, student loan interest and rail fares have used RPI, which usually runs higher than CPI.
Tips and common mistakes
Mixing nominal and real figures. Keep them separate: inflate costs and use nominal returns, or use real returns and today's costs.
Using a single recent year. A spike or dip isn't a good long-run assumption.
Forgetting taxes. Tax is charged on nominal gains, so high inflation can mean tax on gains that are not real.
Assuming wages keep pace. They often do over long periods, but not always in the short run.
Frequently asked questions
What inflation rate should I use?
For long-range planning, many people start with the central banks' 2% target and test 3% or more as a cautious case. For the past, use official CPI data.
How do I calculate what money from the past is worth today?
Multiply the old amount by (1 + rate)^years, using the average inflation rate for that period. Official CPI-based calculators (BLS, Bank of Canada, Bank of England) use actual index values.
What is the difference between CPI and core inflation?
Core measures leave out volatile items, typically food and energy, to show the underlying trend. Headline CPI includes everything.
Why does my own cost of living rise faster than official inflation?
Your spending pattern differs from the average basket. If rent or energy is a big share of your budget and those prices rise quickly, your personal inflation is higher.
Does the calculator use historical data?
Not yet. It applies the constant rate you enter. Historical CPI data for the US, Canada and the UK is planned.
How does inflation affect retirement planning?
It raises the income you'll need. The retirement calculator can show results in today's money.
