H1: Percent Error Calculator
Percent error shows how far a measured or estimated value is from the accepted (true) value, as a percentage of the true value. It's a standard way to judge accuracy in science labs, engineering and estimation. Enter the observed and accepted values to get the percent error and the absolute error.
How to use this calculator
1. Observed (experimental) value: what you measured or estimated.
2. Accepted (true) value: the reference value. It can't be zero.
Results: percent error and absolute error.
The formula
Absolute error = |Observed − Accepted|
Percent error  = |Observed − Accepted| ÷ |Accepted| × 100
The calculator reports the size of the error (always positive). Some courses use a signed version without the absolute value bars, where a negative result means the measurement was too low.
Worked examples
Example 1: measuring gravity
A pendulum experiment gives g = 9.6 m/s². The accepted value is 9.81 m/s².
Absolute error = |9.6 − 9.81| = 0.210000 m/s²
Percent error = 0.21 ÷ 9.81 × 100 = 2.1407%
Example 2: density of aluminium
A student measures 2.82 g/cm³; the accepted value is 2.70 g/cm³.
Percent error = 0.12 ÷ 2.70 × 100 = 4.4444% (signed: +4.44%, an overestimate)
Example 3: boiling point
Water boils at 98.2 °C in a lab where the reference is 100 °C.
Percent error = 1.8000%
(Altitude and pressure lower the boiling point, so the "accepted" value should match your conditions.)

Understanding your results
What's acceptable depends on the field. In a school lab, under 5% is often considered good; precision engineering needs far smaller errors.
Accuracy vs precision: percent error measures accuracy (closeness to truth). Repeated measurements that agree closely are precise even if they're all wrong; the standard deviation calculator measures precision.
Sources of error: instrument resolution, calibration, reaction time, environmental conditions and calculation mistakes.
Tips and common mistakes
Dividing by the observed value instead of the accepted value.
Forgetting to multiply by 100.
Rounding intermediate results too early. Round the final answer to suit your data's significant figures; see the rounding calculator.
Using an accepted value of zero. Percent error is undefined; report the absolute error instead.
Notes for US, Canada and UK readers
School science labs in all three countries use percent error to compare a measured value with an accepted one. A classic is measuring g with a pendulum: a result of 9.6 m/s² against 9.81 m/s² is a percent error of 2.14%. UK A-level practical work also asks for "percentage uncertainty", which is a different idea: it describes the precision of the measurement itself, not its distance from an accepted value.
Frequently asked questions
How do I calculate percent error?
Subtract the accepted value from the observed value, take the absolute value, divide by the accepted value and multiply by 100.
Can percent error be negative?
In the signed version, yes; this calculator shows the magnitude.
What is a good percent error?
It depends on the context; under 5% is a common rule of thumb for school experiments.
What's the difference between percent error and percent difference?
Percent error compares with a true value; percent difference compares two measurements.
Why does the accepted value matter so much?
It's the denominator, so a small accepted value magnifies the percent error.
Can percent error exceed 100%?
Yes, if the measurement is more than double the accepted value, or the wrong sign.
