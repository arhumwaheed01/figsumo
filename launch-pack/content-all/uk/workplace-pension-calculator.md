H1: UK Workplace Pension Calculator
If you work in the UK, are aged 22 to State Pension age and earn more than £10,000 a year, your employer must automatically enrol you in a workplace pension and pay into it. This calculator shows how much you, your employer and the government put in each year and projects what your pension pot could grow to by retirement.
How to use this calculator
1. Annual salary: before tax.
2. Contribution basis: many schemes use "qualifying earnings" (pay between £6,240 and £50,270); some use your full salary or basic pay.
3. Your contribution (%) and employer contribution (%).
4. Tax relief method: relief at source, net pay arrangement or salary sacrifice. Your payslip or scheme documents say which.
5. Current pension pot, years to retirement, expected growth and annual charges.
Results: annual contributions (you, employer, tax relief), cost to your take-home pay, and the projected pot in today's money and future pounds.

Workers earning between £6,240 and £10,000 can ask to join and get the employer contribution; those below £6,240 can ask to join, but the employer doesn't have to contribute.
How it works
Qualifying earnings = min(Salary, 50,270) − 6,240
Employer = Employer % × Qualifying earnings
Employee (gross) = Employee % × Qualifying earnings
Tax relief:
Relief at source: you pay 80% of your contribution from take-home pay and the provider claims 20% basic-rate relief from HMRC. Higher-rate taxpayers claim the extra through Self Assessment.
Net pay arrangement: the contribution is taken before Income Tax, so you get relief at your marginal rate automatically. Very low earners below the Personal Allowance get no relief this way.
Salary sacrifice: you also save National Insurance; see the salary sacrifice calculator.
Projection: the pot grows each year by the expected return minus charges, with contributions added. Results in today's money are deflated by an assumed inflation rate.
Worked examples
Example 1: £30,000 salary at the minimum
Qualifying earnings = 30,000 − 6,240 = £23,760.00
Employer 3% = £712.80
Your 5% = £1,188.00 gross. Under relief at source you pay £950.40 and HMRC adds £237.60.
Total going in: £1,900.80 a year
Kept up for 30 years with 5% annual growth after charges (and no pay rises), that builds a pot of about £126,286.96 from contributions of £57,024.00. In today's money, assuming 2.5% inflation, that's roughly £60,206.39.
Example 2: 5% + 5% on full salary
If your employer instead pays 5% and you pay 5% of your full £30,000, contributions are £3,000.00 a year, and the 30-year projection rises to £199,316.54, £73,029.58 more than the minimum. Check how your scheme defines pensionable pay.
Earning above £50,270
Qualifying earnings stop at £50,270, so at the minimum a high earner's employer pays 3% of £44,030.00, or £1,320.90. Many employers pay more, and some match extra employee contributions; it's worth asking.
Example 3: £45,000 salary at the minimum, qualifying earnings basis
Qualifying earnings = 45,000 − 6,240 = £38,760.00
Your 5% = £1,938.00 gross; under relief at source you pay £1,550.40
Employer 3% = £1,162.80
Total going in: £3,100.80 a year
Example 4: what charges cost
£3,000 a year for 30 years growing at 5% builds about £199,316.54. With 0.75% a year in charges (growth of 4.25%), the pot is about £175,456.59, £23,859.95 less. Check your scheme's annual management charge; auto-enrolment default funds are capped at 0.75% a year.

Understanding your results
Projections aren't guarantees. Investment returns vary, and charges reduce growth. A difference of 0.5% a year in charges makes a large difference over decades.
Tax-free cash: usually 25% of the pot can be taken tax-free from age 55 (rising to 57 in 2028); on a £126,286.96 pot that's £31,571.74. The rest is taxed as income when withdrawn.
State Pension is separate and based on your National Insurance record.
Annual allowance: £60,000 a year across all pensions, tapered for very high incomes.
Opting out
You can opt out within a month of being enrolled and get your contributions back, but you lose the employer contribution and tax relief. Employers must re-enrol you about every three years.
Tips and common mistakes
Opting out to boost take-home pay. You give up free money from your employer.
Forgetting old pensions. Use the government's Pension Tracing Service to find lost pots.
Not claiming higher-rate relief on relief-at-source schemes.
Ignoring charges and fund choice. The default fund suits many people, but check it.
Frequently asked questions
How much do I have to pay into a workplace pension?
At the minimum, 8% of qualifying earnings in total, with at least 3% from your employer.
Who gets auto-enrolled?
Workers aged 22 to State Pension age earning over £10,000 a year.
What are qualifying earnings?
Earnings between £6,240 and £50,270 a year in 2026/27.
Can my employer pay less than 3%?
Not if it uses qualifying earnings for auto-enrolment.
When can I take money from my pension?
From age 55 (57 from 2028), with 25% usually tax-free.
What happens to my pension if I change jobs?
It stays invested. You can leave it or transfer it to a new scheme.
