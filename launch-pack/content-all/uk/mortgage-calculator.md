H1: UK Mortgage Calculator
This calculator estimates monthly repayments on a UK mortgage in pounds sterling. Enter the property price, your deposit, the rate and the term. You can compare a repayment mortgage with interest-only, see how much an overpayment saves, and check what happens to the payment when an introductory fixed rate ends.
How to use this calculator
1. Property price and deposit. The calculator shows your loan-to-value (LTV). Lenders price deals in LTV bands, so a bigger deposit often gets a cheaper rate.
2. Interest rate and fixed period. Enter the initial rate and how long it lasts, for example 2 or 5 years. Then enter the rate you expect afterwards, usually the lender's standard variable rate (SVR) or a new deal. If you are unsure, leave the follow-on rate equal to the initial rate.
3. Term. The total length of the mortgage, commonly 25 to 35 years.
4. Repayment type. Choose repayment (capital and interest), interest-only, or part-and-part.
5. Overpayments (optional). Add a monthly or one-off overpayment. Many fixed-rate deals charge an early repayment charge (ERC) above a yearly overpayment limit. Enter your lender's limit if you know it, and the calculator will warn you when you go over it.
The formula
For a repayment mortgage with interest charged monthly:
M = P × r / (1 − (1 + r)^−n)
M: monthly repayment
P: amount borrowed (price − deposit)
r: monthly rate = annual rate ÷ 12
n: number of monthly payments (term in years × 12)
For interest-only:
M = P × annual rate ÷ 12
The balance does not fall, and the full amount is still owed at the end of the term.
When the fixed period ends, the calculator takes the remaining balance and recalculates M with the new rate over the remaining months.
Some lenders calculate interest daily rather than monthly. This changes the payment by pennies, not pounds.
Worked examples
Example 1: repayment vs interest-only. Price £312,500, deposit £62,500 (20%), loan £250,000 (LTV 80%), 4.5%, 25 years.
r = 0.045 / 12 = 0.00375; n = 300
Repayment: £1,389.58 a month, with total interest over 25 years of £166,874.36
Interest-only: £250,000 × 0.045 ÷ 12 = £937.50 a month. That is £452.08 less each month, but after 25 years you still owe £250,000 and need a plan to repay it.
Longer term: the same loan over 35 years costs £1,183.14 a month, but total interest rises to £246,919.57.
Example 2: when the fix ends. The loan is fixed at 4.5% for 2 years, then moves to a follow-on rate of 6.5% (an illustrative figure; use your lender's actual rate).
After 24 payments of £1,389.58, the balance is £238,669.02
Recalculated over the remaining 276 months at 6.5%: £1,668.45 a month, a rise of £278.87
Seeing this rise in advance is the main reason to plan a remortgage before a fixed rate expires.
Overpaying: an extra £100 a month from the start on the 25-year, 4.5% loan clears it in 266 months instead of 300 (2 years 10 months sooner) and saves £21,870.77 in interest.
Understanding your results
Monthly repayment is the mortgage payment only. Buildings insurance, any service charge or ground rent, and council tax are separate.
LTV shows which pricing band you fall into. Moving from 90% to 85%, or from 80% to 75%, can unlock cheaper rates. Check real deals.
Total interest assumes rates follow what you entered. In reality, you will probably move to several different deals over the term.
Remaining balance at the end of the fix is the figure a remortgage would need to cover, plus any fees.
Common UK mortgage types
Fixed rate: the rate is fixed for a set period, often 2, 3 or 5 years, then reverts to the lender's follow-on rate unless you remortgage.
Tracker: the rate moves with a reference rate, usually the Bank of England Bank Rate, plus a set margin. Payments rise and fall with it.
Discount: a discount off the lender's standard variable rate for a period. The lender can change the SVR, so the payment can change.
Standard variable rate (SVR): the lender's own variable rate, typically what you move to after a deal ends.
Offset: savings held with the lender reduce the balance that interest is charged on. To model one, enter the loan minus your savings.
For a tracker or variable deal, run the calculator at a few different rates to see the range of payments you might face.

Tips and common mistakes
Ignoring product fees. A lower rate with a £999 fee can cost more than a higher fee-free rate on a small loan.
Forgetting the deal period ends. Diarise remortgaging before you move onto the lender's SVR.
Comparing interest-only and repayment payments as like for like. Interest-only leaves the full loan to repay.
Overlooking early repayment charges. They often apply during the fixed period.
Leaving out Stamp Duty and legal costs. Budget for them alongside the deposit.
Frequently asked questions
What is the difference between repayment and interest-only?
On a repayment mortgage, every payment covers that month's interest plus some of the capital, so the debt reaches zero at the end of the term. On interest-only, you pay just the interest and must repay the whole loan at the end, for example from savings, investments or a sale.
How is LTV calculated?
Loan ÷ property value × 100. A £250,000 loan on a £312,500 property is an LTV of 80%.
What happens when my fixed rate ends?
Unless you switch to a new deal, most lenders move you to their standard variable rate, which is often higher. The calculator recalculates the payment on your remaining balance at the follow-on rate you enter.
Can I overpay my mortgage?
Most lenders allow some overpayment. Fixed-rate deals often limit how much you can overpay each year before an early repayment charge applies. Check your mortgage offer for the exact limit.
Does a longer term save money?
It lowers the monthly payment but increases total interest. In Example 1, stretching from 25 to 35 years cuts the payment by £206.44 a month but adds £80,045.21 of interest.
Is the rate the same as the APRC?
No. The APRC (annual percentage rate of charge) includes fees and the follow-on rate across the whole term, so you can compare deals. Use the initial rate and the follow-on rate here.
Does this include stamp duty or fees?
No. Arrangement fees, valuation and legal costs are separate. Stamp duty has its own calculator.
