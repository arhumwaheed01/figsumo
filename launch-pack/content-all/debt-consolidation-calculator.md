H1: Debt Consolidation Calculator
Debt consolidation replaces several debts with one new loan, ideally at a lower interest rate. This calculator compares your current path (keep paying what you pay now until the debts are gone) with taking a consolidation loan for a set term. It shows the new monthly payment, how long your current plan would take, the interest under each option and the estimated saving.
How to use this calculator
1. Total existing debt: the sum of the balances you want to consolidate, such as credit cards, store cards and personal loans.
2. Current weighted APR: the average rate across those debts, weighted by balance (see the example below for how to work it out).
3. Current total monthly payment: what you pay across all of them each month now.
4. Consolidation loan APR: the rate offered on the new loan. Use the APR, so fees are included where possible.
5. New loan term (years): the repayment period of the consolidation loan.
Results: new monthly payment, old payoff time, interest on the current path, interest with consolidation and interest savings (estimate).
The formula
Current path: the calculator steps through each month, charging interest at the weighted APR and subtracting your current payment until the balance reaches zero.
Interest = Balance × (Weighted APR ÷ 12);  Balance = Balance + Interest − Payment
Consolidation loan: a standard fixed payment,
PMT = D × r / (1 − (1 + r)^−n),   interest = PMT × n − D
D: total debt; r: new APR ÷ 12; n: term in months
Savings = current-path interest − consolidation interest
Weighted APR = Σ(balance × APR) ÷ Σ balance.
Worked examples
Example 1: $15,000 at 18%, paying $450 a month, vs a 9.5% 5-year loan
Current path: 47 months, interest $5,950.81
Consolidation: $315.03 a month for 60 months, interest $3,901.68
Estimated saving: $2,049.14, and a lower monthly payment
Note the trade-off: the current path finishes sooner, but costs more interest. If you kept paying $450 a month on the new loan, you would clear it even faster and save more.

Understanding your results
New monthly payment is principal and interest on the consolidation loan.
Old payoff time is how long your current payment would take, assuming no new spending and the weighted APR staying constant.
Interest savings compares total interest only. It is an estimate: individual debts with different rates pay down in a different order in real life, and the weighted APR is an approximation.
When consolidation costs more
A longer term. The same $15,000 at 9.5% over 7 years cuts the payment to $245.16 but costs $5,593.42 of interest, more than the 5-year loan.
Fees. An origination fee of 5% added to the loan raises the payment to $330.78 and interest to $4,846.76 over 5 years. Add any fee to "Total existing debt" if it is financed, or compare APRs.
Running the cards back up. If the cleared cards fill up again, you end up with the loan and new card debt.
Secured consolidation. Using a home equity loan or remortgage can get a low rate, but it turns unsecured debt into debt secured on your home.
Country notes
United States: consolidation is usually a personal loan or a 0% balance transfer card. Non-profit credit counseling agencies offer debt management plans that can lower card rates without a new loan. The CFPB and FTC warn about debt settlement firms charging upfront fees.
Canada: banks and credit unions offer consolidation loans and lines of credit. A consumer proposal through a Licensed Insolvency Trustee is a formal alternative for serious debt.
United Kingdom: consolidation loans are regulated by the FCA, and lenders must check affordability. Free advice from MoneyHelper, StepChange or Citizens Advice can help you choose between a loan, a debt management plan or other options.
Tips and common mistakes
Compare APR and total cost, not just the monthly payment.
Keep the term short enough to beat your current payoff time, if you can afford the payment.
Close or freeze the cleared cards if you are tempted to reuse them, keeping in mind that closing old accounts can affect credit scores.
Check early repayment terms on the old debts and the new loan.
Frequently asked questions
Does debt consolidation hurt your credit score?
Applying causes a hard credit check, which may lower a score slightly for a while. Over time, lower card balances and on-time payments can help.
How do I calculate a weighted APR?
Multiply each balance by its APR, add the results, and divide by the total balance.
Is a lower monthly payment a good sign?
Not on its own. A lower payment over a longer term can cost more in total. Look at the interest figures.
What credit score do I need for a consolidation loan?
Lenders set their own criteria. Better credit usually means a lower rate; prequalification tools can show likely rates without a hard check.
Should I use a balance transfer card instead?
If the balance can be cleared within the 0% period and the transfer fee is lower than the interest saved, it can be cheaper. After the promotion, the rate jumps.
Can I consolidate student loans with this?
You can model the numbers, but government student loans have their own consolidation programs and protections that private loans do not.
