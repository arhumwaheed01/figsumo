H1: BTU Calculator
BTU (British thermal unit) per hour is how air conditioners, heat pumps and heaters are rated. Buy a unit that's too small and it runs constantly without keeping up; too large and it cycles on and off, wastes energy and leaves the room damp. This calculator estimates the capacity a room needs from its length, width, ceiling height and how demanding its conditions are.
How to use this calculator
1. Room length (ft) and room width (ft): inside wall to wall (default 14 × 12).
2. Ceiling height (ft): default 8.
3. Climate factor: mild, average, or cold / sunny.
Results: estimated BTU/hr and room volume (ft³).

Higher ceilings mean more air to cool, so scale up proportionally (a 10 ft ceiling is 25% more volume than 8 ft).
Worked examples
Example 1: a bedroom
14 × 12 ft = 168 ft² (volume 1,344 ft³) → 6,000 BTU/hr. Very sunny: 6,600. With four people regularly using it: 6,000 + 2 × 600 = 7,200.
Example 2: a kitchen-diner
20 × 16 ft = 320 ft² → 8,000 BTU/hr, plus 4,000 for the kitchen = 12,000 BTU/hr.
Example 3: converting units
6,000 BTU/hr is about 1.76 kW of cooling. One "ton" of cooling is 12,000 BTU/hr, so a 2-ton system delivers 24,000 BTU/hr.
Heating vs cooling
Heating needs depend far more on climate, insulation and how warm you want the room compared with outside. Rough rules of thumb range from about 20 BTU/hr per ft² in mild climates with good insulation to 50–60 in cold climates with poor insulation [VERIFY for your climate zone]. For whole-house systems, installers in the US and Canada should carry out a Manual J load calculation; in the UK, heating engineers do a room-by-room heat-loss survey.
Understanding your results
It's an estimate. Windows, insulation, appliances and orientation matter.
Round to the nearest available unit size, usually the next size up if you're between two.
Portable units are often rated under a newer standard (SACC) that gives lower BTU figures than the older ASHRAE rating; compare like with like.
Running cost: use the electricity calculator with the unit's wattage.
Tips and common mistakes
Oversizing. A unit that's too big cools quickly without removing humidity, leaving the room clammy.
Ignoring open-plan spaces. Include connected areas without doors.
Measuring outside walls instead of the room's interior.
Forgetting heat sources such as computers, TVs and cooking.
Frequently asked questions
How many BTUs do I need for a 12 × 12 room?
144 ft² sits in the 100–150 band: about 5,000 BTU/hr, more if very sunny.
What does BTU stand for?
British thermal unit: the heat needed to raise one pound of water by 1 °F.
How many BTUs per square foot?
Roughly 20–40 BTU/hr per ft² for cooling, depending on room size.
Is a bigger air conditioner better?
No. It short-cycles and doesn't dehumidify well.
How do I convert BTU to kW?
Multiply BTU/hr by 0.000293.
What's a ton of air conditioning?
12,000 BTU/hr.
