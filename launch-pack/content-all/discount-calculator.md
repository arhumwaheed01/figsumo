H1: Discount Calculator
This discount calculator gives the sale price and the amount you save when an item is reduced by a percentage. Enter the original price and the discount percent. It is quick for checking shelf tags, comparing deals, or working out a price for a sale you are running.
How to use this calculator
1. Original price: the regular price before the discount, without tax.
2. Discount: the percentage off, e.g. 25 for "25% off".
Results: sale price and you save (the discount in money).
The formula
You save   = Original price × Discount% ÷ 100
Sale price = Original price − You save  =  Original price × (1 − Discount% ÷ 100)
Worked examples
Example 1: $89.99 at 25% off
You save = 89.99 × 0.25 = $22.50
Sale price = 89.99 − 22.50 = $67.49
(The saving is 22.4975, which rounds to $22.50.)
Example 2: stacked discounts
A $120 jacket is 30% off, and you have a coupon for an extra 10% off the sale price.
After 30%: 120 × 0.70 = $84.00
After the extra 10%: 84 × 0.90 = $75.60
The combined discount is 37%, not 40%, because the second discount applies to the already-reduced price.
To do this in the calculator, run it twice: first with 30%, then enter the sale price as the new original price with 10%. If 8.25% sales tax applies at the register, the final cost is $81.84.
Example 3: "$15 off" or "20% off"?
On an $80 purchase:
$15 off: you pay $65.00
20% off: 80 × 0.80 = $64.00
The percentage wins here by $1.00. The break-even price is 15 ÷ 0.20 = $75: below $75, the fixed $15 is the better deal.
Example 4: a discount and then tax in Ontario
A $150 item at 20% off costs $120.00 before tax. Ontario's 13% HST is charged on the discounted price: 120 × 1.13 = $135.60.
Example 5: a UK sale price with VAT included
A £60 item (VAT included) is 25% off, so you pay £45.00. The VAT inside that price is 45 ÷ 6 = £7.50, because at the 20% standard rate VAT is one sixth of a VAT-inclusive price.

Understanding your results
Sale price is before sales tax or VAT, unless the original price already included tax (as UK and EU shelf prices do).
You save is the money discount. Big percentages on cheap items may save less than small percentages on expensive ones.
Other discount questions, solved by hand
Find the original price from a sale price: original = sale price ÷ (1 − discount). A $51 item marked "40% off" was originally $85.00.
Find the percent off from two prices: discount% = (original − sale) ÷ original × 100. $60 reduced by $15 is 25% off.
Buy one, get one free: you pay for one item and get two, an effective 50% discount per item, but only if you wanted two.
Buy one, get one half price: 25% off the pair.
Country notes
United States: shelf prices usually exclude sales tax, which is added at checkout on the discounted price. Manufacturer coupons can be taxed differently from store coupons in some states [VERIFY your state's rule].
Canada: prices are usually shown before GST/HST and PST, which are charged on the discounted price.
United Kingdom: displayed prices for consumers must include VAT. "Was/now" price claims must be genuine under the CMA's pricing guidance and consumer protection law; a "was" price should be one at which the item was actually sold for a meaningful period.
Tips and common mistakes
Adding stacked discounts together. 20% + 20% is 36% off, not 40%.
Comparing percent with amount off. "$10 off" beats "15% off" only for prices under about $66.67.
Forgetting shipping. A bigger discount can be cancelled out by delivery charges.
Trusting inflated "was" prices. Check the price history if you can.
Discounting tax-inclusive prices. In the UK, the discount applies to the VAT-inclusive price, and the VAT is reduced proportionally.
If you're the one offering the discount
A discount cuts profit by more than the percentage suggests. Suppose an item sells for $100 and costs you $60, leaving $40 of gross profit:
At 20% off, the price is $80 and the profit $20, half what it was.
To earn the same $40 total profit, you'd need to sell 2 items instead of 1.
At 10% off, profit is $30, so you'd need 1.33 times the sales volume to break even.
The rule: required sales multiple = old margin ÷ (old margin − discount), with both as percentages of the original price. With a 40% margin and a 20% discount, 40 ÷ 20 = 2. Check this before running a sale, and count any extra costs such as card fees and shipping.
Frequently asked questions
How do I calculate a discount?
Multiply the price by the discount percentage and divide by 100. Subtract that from the price to get the sale price.
How do I calculate 20% off?
Multiply the price by 0.80. For $45, that is $36.
Are two 25% discounts the same as 50% off?
No. Two 25% discounts give 43.75% off (0.75 × 0.75 = 0.5625 of the price).
How do I work out the original price?
Divide the sale price by (1 − discount as a decimal). For 30% off, divide by 0.70.
Is sales tax charged before or after the discount?
For store discounts, usually after, on the reduced price. Rules for manufacturer coupons and rebates vary.
What is the difference between a discount and a markdown?
A markdown is a permanent price reduction by the seller; a discount can be temporary or tied to a coupon or customer group. The maths is the same.
