H1: Payback Period Calculator
The payback period is how long it takes for an investment's cash returns to repay what it cost. This calculator gives the simple payback period when the investment produces the same cash flow each year. Enter the upfront cost and the expected annual cash inflow (or saving) to get the answer in years and months.
It is a quick screening test for projects such as new equipment, energy-saving upgrades, solar panels or a small business purchase.
How to use this calculator
1. Initial investment: the total upfront cost, including purchase, installation and setup.
2. Annual cash flow: the net cash the investment brings in (or saves) each year, after its own running costs. For an energy upgrade, it is the yearly saving on bills.
Results: simple payback period in years and months (approx.).
The formula
Payback period (years) = Initial investment ÷ Annual cash flow
Months = Years × 12
This assumes the same cash flow every year and ignores the time value of money.
Worked examples
Example 1: $50,000 machine saving $12,000 a year
Payback = 50,000 ÷ 12,000 = 4.17 years, about 50 months
Example 2: uneven cash flows (by hand)
A $40,000 project returns $8,000, $12,000, $15,000, $15,000 and $10,000 in years 1 to 5.

After year 3, $35,000 has been recovered; the remaining $5,000 comes from year 4's $15,000, a third of the way through. Payback ≈ 3.33 years.
Discounted payback
Money received later is worth less. Discounting Example 1's $12,000 a year at 8% (each year's flow divided by 1.08^year), the cumulative discounted cash reaches $50,000 after about 5.28 years, longer than the simple 4.17 years.
Understanding your results
Shorter payback = faster recovery of your money and less exposure to things going wrong.
Payback ignores everything after the payback point. Two projects with the same payback can have very different total returns. Check the total with the ROI calculator or the IRR calculator.
It ignores the time value of money unless you use the discounted version.
Many businesses set a maximum payback (for example 3 years for equipment) as a rough risk filter, alongside other measures.
Uses and examples
Energy upgrades: an $18,000 solar installation that saves $1,400 a year on electricity pays back in about 12.9 years, before any tax credits or rebates. Check government incentives first, because they shorten payback considerably. Estimate your current electricity use with the electricity calculator.
Equipment: compare the payback of buying versus leasing.
Business purchases: how long the profits take to repay the price.
Marketing: customer acquisition cost ÷ monthly margin per customer gives months to recover acquisition spending.

Payback for a home energy upgrade, step by step
1. Get the installed price, then subtract any grant, rebate or tax credit you will definitely receive.
2. Estimate the yearly saving from past bills: for example, kilowatt-hours saved × your price per kWh, or the drop in gas use × your gas price.
3. Subtract any new running costs, such as servicing.
4. Divide the net cost by the net yearly saving.
5. Compare the answer with the equipment's expected life. If payback is close to the end of its life, the upgrade may never pay off financially, although comfort or emissions may still justify it.
Energy prices change, so test a higher and a lower price to see how sensitive the payback is.

Tips and common mistakes
Using revenue instead of net cash flow. Subtract running costs.
Forgetting maintenance and replacement costs that occur during the payback period.
Ignoring incentives and tax effects, which change both the cost and the cash flows.
Treating payback as the only test. Use it with ROI or NPV.
Frequently asked questions
What is a good payback period?
It depends on the type of investment and your risk tolerance. Shorter is safer; many businesses look for payback well within the asset's useful life.
How do I calculate payback with uneven cash flows?
Add the yearly flows until the total reaches the investment, then work out the fraction of the final year needed, as in Example 2.
What is discounted payback?
Payback calculated with each year's cash flow discounted to present value. It is always longer than simple payback.
Does payback account for profit after it is reached?
No. That is its main weakness.
Can the payback period be infinite?
If the annual cash flow is zero or negative, the investment never pays back.
Should I include tax?
For a realistic figure, use after-tax cash flows and include any tax credits.
How is payback different from break-even?
Break-even finds the sales volume at which revenue covers costs in a period. Payback finds the time it takes for cumulative cash to repay an upfront investment. See the break-even calculator.
