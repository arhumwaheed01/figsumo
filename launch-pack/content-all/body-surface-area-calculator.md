H1: Body Surface Area Calculator
Body surface area (BSA) is the total area of the outer surface of the body, in square metres. Doctors and pharmacists use it to dose some medicines, especially chemotherapy, to index measurements such as cardiac output and kidney function, and to estimate fluid needs in burns care. This calculator estimates BSA from height and weight using the Mosteller formula.
How to use this calculator
1. Units: metric (kg, cm) or imperial (lb, ft and in).
2. Weight.
3. Height.
Result: body surface area (Mosteller) in m².
The formula
Mosteller (1987):
BSA (m²) = √(Height (cm) × Weight (kg) ÷ 3,600)
It's the most widely used formula because it's simple and agrees closely with older formulas for most adults.
Other formulas you may see:
Du Bois and Du Bois (1916): BSA = 0.007184 × W^0.425 × H^0.725
Haycock (1978), often used for children: BSA = 0.024265 × W^0.5378 × H^0.3964
Worked examples
Example 1: adult, 70 kg and 175 cm
175 × 70 = 12,250; ÷ 3,600 and square root
BSA = 1.84 m² (Mosteller)
Du Bois gives 1.85 m², Haycock 1.85 m²: very close for an average adult.

Understanding your results
Typical adult values are around 1.6 to 2.0 m²; the commonly quoted "average" is about 1.7 to 1.9 m².
Medication dosing: some drugs are prescribed in mg per m². For a drug dosed at 75 mg/m², a person with a BSA of 1.84 m² would be prescribed about 138 mg. Clinicians may cap or adjust doses, so never calculate a dose yourself.
Indexing: cardiac index (cardiac output ÷ BSA) and estimated GFR reported "per 1.73 m²" use BSA to compare people of different sizes. See the GFR calculator.
Formula differences are small for typical adults but larger at extremes of size, in young children and in people with obesity.
Using BSA-indexed values
Cardiac index: a cardiac output of 5.0 L/min in a person with a BSA of 1.84 m² gives a cardiac index of 5.0 ÷ 1.84 = 2.72 L/min/m². A commonly cited normal range is about 2.5 to 4.0 L/min/m².
Kidney function: laboratory eGFR is reported per 1.73 m². To convert an eGFR of 60 to an absolute value for someone with a BSA of 1.84 m²: 60 × 1.84 ÷ 1.73 = 63.8 mL/min. Some drug-dosing guidance uses the absolute value, which is a decision for the prescriber.
Notes for US, Canada and UK users
BSA-based dosing is used in cancer care in all three countries, but local policies differ on details such as which formula to use and whether to cap the BSA for very large patients. In the US, the American Society of Clinical Oncology recommends full weight-based chemotherapy doses for most patients with obesity rather than routinely capping BSA at 2.0 m². UK cancer networks and Canadian provincial cancer agencies publish their own protocols, which hospital pharmacists follow [VERIFY current local guidance]. Whatever your country, a BSA you calculate at home is for interest only.
When BSA is less useful
BSA doesn't distinguish fat from muscle, so in people with obesity it may overestimate the body mass that drugs actually distribute into; clinicians sometimes use adjusted body weight or other approaches. For burns, the percentage of BSA burned is usually estimated from charts such as the "rule of nines" or Lund and Browder charts rather than from this calculation.
Tips and common mistakes
Entering height in metres instead of centimetres (175, not 1.75).
Using pounds in a metric formula. Convert first or switch units.
Using an outdated weight for anything clinical.
Frequently asked questions
What is a normal body surface area?
For adults, roughly 1.6 to 2.0 m², depending on size.
Which BSA formula is best?
Mosteller is the most common and simplest. Hospitals may specify a particular formula for consistency.
Why is BSA used for chemotherapy?
BSA correlates with metabolic rate and drug clearance better than weight alone for some drugs.
How do I calculate BSA by hand?
Multiply height (cm) by weight (kg), divide by 3,600 and take the square root.
Is BSA the same as BMI?
No. BMI relates weight to height squared to screen weight status; BSA estimates surface area.
Can I use this for children?
Yes, the formula works for children, but paediatric dosing must be done by clinicians.
