H1: CD Calculator
A certificate of deposit (CD) pays a fixed rate for a fixed term. This calculator shows what your deposit will be worth at maturity and how much interest it earns, using the annual percentage yield (APY) the bank quotes. Enter the deposit, the APY and the term in months.
How to use this calculator
1. Initial deposit: the amount you put in when you open the CD.
2. Annual APY: the annual percentage yield on the bank's rate sheet. APY already includes the effect of compounding, so you don't need to know how often the bank compounds.
3. Term (months): the CD's length, e.g. 6, 12, 18, 24 or 60 months.
Results: maturity value and interest earned.
The formula
Because APY is the effective annual rate, growth for any term is:
Maturity value = P × (1 + APY)^(m ÷ 12)
Interest earned = Maturity value − P
P: initial deposit
APY: annual percentage yield as a decimal (4.25% = 0.0425)
m: term in months
This assumes interest stays in the CD until maturity. If you have the nominal rate (APR) instead, convert first: APY = (1 + APR ÷ k)^k − 1, where k is the number of compounding periods per year.
Worked examples
Example 1: $10,000 in a 12-month CD at 4.25% APY
Maturity value = 10,000 × 1.0425 = $10,425.00
Interest earned = $425.00
For a 12-month term, APY gives the interest directly: 4.25% of $10,000.
Example 2: $25,000 in a 30-month CD at 4.00% APY
Maturity value = 25,000 × 1.04^(30 ÷ 12) = 25,000 × 1.04^2.5 = $27,575.50
Interest earned = $2,575.50
A bank compounding monthly would need a nominal rate of 3.928% to produce a 4.00% APY
Understanding your results
Maturity value is what you'd receive at the end if you leave the money untouched and the bank compounds as the APY assumes.
Interest earned is before tax. CD interest is taxable as income in the US, Canada and the UK unless held in a tax-advantaged account.
Real return: if inflation is 3%, a 4.25% APY is a real return of about 1.21%. Use the inflation calculator to see buying power.
Interest paid out? Some CDs pay interest monthly to your checking account instead of compounding. Then the total interest is slightly lower than shown.
Early withdrawal penalties
Most CDs charge a penalty for withdrawing before maturity, often expressed as a number of months' interest. For example, a penalty of six months' interest on a $25,000 CD at about 4% would be roughly $500.00. Penalties can exceed the interest earned if you withdraw very early, cutting into your deposit. Read the account terms before you open the CD.
Ways to use CDs
CD ladder: split money across CDs that mature at different times (e.g. 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 years). Each year one matures, giving access to cash and a chance to reinvest at current rates.
Matching a goal: pick a term that ends when you need the money, such as a house deposit next year.
Rate lock: when rates are expected to fall, a longer CD locks in today's rate. When rates may rise, shorter terms keep flexibility.
CD or high-yield savings account?
A savings account lets you withdraw at any time, but its rate can change whenever the bank decides. A CD fixes the rate but locks the money away. If the CD's APY is only slightly above a good savings rate, the flexibility of the savings account may be worth more. If the gap is large and you are sure you won't need the money, the CD's guaranteed rate wins. You can model the savings account side with the savings calculator.
Country notes
United States: CDs at FDIC-insured banks are covered up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category; credit union share certificates are covered by the NCUA on the same basis. Interest is reported on Form 1099-INT.
Canada: the equivalent product is a GIC (guaranteed investment certificate). Eligible deposits at CDIC member institutions are covered up to $100,000 per category. GICs can be held in a TFSA or RRSP to shelter the interest.
United Kingdom: the equivalent is a fixed-rate bond or fixed-term savings account. Banks quote an AER, which works like APY. The FSCS protects up to £120,000 per person per authorised bank from 1 December 2025 (previously £85,000) [VERIFY]. Interest within the Personal Savings Allowance (£1,000 basic rate, £500 higher rate) is tax-free, or use a cash ISA.
Tips and common mistakes
Mixing APY and APR. Enter the APY. If the bank only shows a rate and compounding frequency, convert first.
Ignoring penalties. Only lock away money you won't need before maturity.
Watching automatic renewal. Many CDs roll over into a new term at the bank's current rate unless you act during the grace period.
Exceeding insurance limits. Spread large sums across banks or ownership categories.
Frequently asked questions
What is the difference between APY and interest rate?
The interest rate (APR) is the nominal yearly rate. APY includes compounding, showing what you actually earn in a year. For comparing CDs, use APY.
How often do CDs compound?
It varies by bank: daily, monthly or quarterly. Because APY already reflects compounding, the calculator gives the right answer whichever the bank uses.
Is CD interest taxable?
Generally yes, as income in the year it is credited, even if you don't withdraw it, unless the CD is inside a tax-advantaged account.
Can I lose money in a CD?
Not from rate changes: the rate is fixed. You can lose some principal if an early withdrawal penalty exceeds the interest earned, and inflation can erode buying power.
What happens at maturity?
You can withdraw, move the money or let it renew. Banks give a short grace period, often around 7 to 10 days, to decide.
Are brokered CDs different?
Brokered CDs are bought through a brokerage and can be sold before maturity on a secondary market, where their price can be above or below what you paid.
