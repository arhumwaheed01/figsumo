H1: Present Value Calculator
Present value (PV) tells you what a future sum is worth in today's money, given a rate of return you could earn in the meantime (the discount rate). It's the reverse of future value. Enter the future amount, the discount rate and the number of years to see the present value.
How to use this calculator
1. Future value: the amount you'll receive (or need) in the future.
2. Discount rate: the annual rate used to bring the amount back to today. Use the return you could reasonably earn on money of similar risk, or your borrowing rate if you'd otherwise borrow.
3. Years: how far in the future the amount is.
Result: present value. The tool discounts monthly: it divides the annual rate by 12 and discounts over years × 12 months.
The formula
PV = FV ÷ (1 + r ÷ 12)^(12 × t)
FV: future value
r: annual discount rate as a decimal
t: years
With annual discounting the formula is PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)^t, which gives a slightly higher PV. At a 0% rate, PV equals FV.
Worked examples
Example 1: $100,000 in 10 years at 6%
PV = 100,000 ÷ (1 + 0.06 ÷ 12)^120 = $54,963.27
With annual discounting: $55,839.48

Example 2: how much to set aside today
You'll need $25,000 for a car in 5 years and can earn 4% (compounded monthly).
PV = 25,000 ÷ (1 + 0.04 ÷ 12)^60 = $20,475.08
That's the lump sum to invest now.
Example 3: lump sum or annual payments?
A prize offers $1,000,000 now or $60,000 a year for 20 years, paid at the end of each year. At a 5% discount rate, the payments are worth 60,000 × (1 − 1.05⁻²⁰) ÷ 0.05 = $747,732.62 today, so the lump sum is worth more, before tax. At a 2% rate, the payments would be worth $981,086.00, and the choice flips. The answer depends entirely on the return you can realistically earn.
Example 4: a future cost in today's money
Expecting a $50,000 expense in 20 years and assuming 3% inflation a year, the amount in today's purchasing power is 50,000 ÷ 1.03²⁰ = $27,683.79 (annual compounding). Here the "discount rate" is inflation, not an investment return.

Present value of a series of payments
For regular equal payments, use the annuity formula:
PV = PMT × (1 − (1 + i)^−n) ÷ i
$1,000 a month for 10 years at 5% (i = 0.05 ÷ 12, n = 120) has a present value of $94,281.35.
A prize paid as $50,000 a year for 20 years at a 5% discount rate is worth $623,110.52 today if paid at the end of each year, or $654,266.04 if the first payment comes immediately. Compare that with any lump-sum alternative.
The current tool handles single amounts; add up the PV of separate amounts, or use the formula above for a level series.
Understanding your results
Present value is the amount that, invested today at the discount rate, would grow to the future value.
The discount rate is a judgment. It should reflect what you'd give up (opportunity cost) and the risk that the future money doesn't arrive.
Inflation: discounting at a nominal rate gives a nominal PV. To think in purchasing power, use the inflation calculator or a real discount rate.
Where present value is used
Comparing a lump sum with payments: pensions, lottery prizes, legal settlements and structured payouts.
Valuing investments: bonds (see the bond calculator), rental property and businesses are valued by discounting future cash flows.
Net present value (NPV): subtract the cost of an investment from the PV of its returns. Positive NPV means it beats your discount rate; see the IRR calculator.
Savings targets: how much to put aside now for a future cost.
Country notes
The maths is the same everywhere. Discount rates used in official contexts differ: the US Office of Management and Budget publishes discount rates for federal project analysis (Circular A-4/A-94), and HM Treasury's Green Book sets a 3.5% social time preference rate for UK public projects. For personal decisions, use your own opportunity cost.
Tips and common mistakes
Mismatched compounding. This tool discounts monthly; textbooks often use annual. State which you're using.
Using an unrealistically high rate makes future money look worthless; too low overstates it.
Ignoring risk. A promised payment that may not arrive deserves a higher discount rate.
Forgetting taxes on the future amount.
Quick check
Match the discount rate's period to the cash-flow period.
Frequently asked questions
What is present value?
The value today of money you'll receive in the future, after discounting at a chosen rate.
What discount rate should I use?
The return you could earn on an alternative with similar risk. For safe amounts, a high-quality savings or government bond rate; for risky ones, higher.
Why is present value less than future value?
Because money today can earn a return. To end up with the future amount, you need less than that amount now.
How do I find the present value of monthly payments?
Use PV = PMT × (1 − (1 + i)^−n) ÷ i with the monthly rate and number of payments.
Does the calculator compound annually or monthly?
Monthly. For annual discounting, use FV ÷ (1 + r)^t.
What is the difference between PV and NPV?
PV is the value of future cash; NPV subtracts what you pay today.
