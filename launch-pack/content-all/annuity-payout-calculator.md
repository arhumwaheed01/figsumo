H1: Annuity Payout Calculator
This calculator turns a lump sum into a level monthly income. Enter the balance you will draw from, the annual return you expect it to earn while it is being paid out, and the number of years the income should last. The result is the fixed monthly amount that runs the balance down to exactly zero at the end of that period.
It suits a retirement pot, an annuity balance at the point you start withdrawals, an inheritance you want to spread out, or any fund you plan to spend evenly.
How to use this calculator
1. Account balance: the amount available on the day payouts start. Take it from your latest annuity, 401(k), IRA, RRSP/RRIF or pension statement, after any tax you must pay on a lump-sum withdrawal.
2. Annual return: the yearly rate the remaining balance earns during the payout years. A lower, cautious figure is sensible because the money is being spent, not left to grow.
3. Payout period (years): how many years the income must last.
The results are the monthly payout (level) and the total paid out over the whole period.
The formula
The payout is the payment on an amortizing balance, like a loan in reverse:
Payout = B × r / (1 − (1 + r)^−n)
B: starting balance
r: monthly return = annual return ÷ 12
n: number of monthly payouts = years × 12
Conventions: payouts are made at the end of each month, and the balance earns interest monthly at r. At 0% return, the payout is simply B ÷ n.
Each month, the balance earns interest and then the payout is taken. In the early years, most of each payment is covered by interest; later, more of it comes from capital.
Worked examples
Example 1: $500,000 over 25 years at 5%
r = 0.05 ÷ 12; n = 300
Payout = 500,000 × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−300) = $2,922.95 a month
Total paid out: $876,885.06, of which $376,885.06 is investment growth earned during the payout years
First month: the balance earns $2,083.33 of interest, so only $839.62 of the first payout comes from capital
Example 2: $250,000 over 20 years at 4%
n = 240, r = 0.04 ÷ 12
Payout = $1,514.95 a month; total $363,588.20
Stretching the same balance to 30 years lowers the payout to $1,193.54
With no growth at all (0%), 20 years of payouts would be $1,041.67 a month
Working the other way: if you take $2,000 a month from $250,000 at 4%, the money lasts about 162.0 months (13.5 years). The calculator does not yet solve for duration; the formula is n = −ln(1 − B × r ÷ payout) ÷ ln(1 + r).
Understanding your results
Monthly payout (level) is constant in dollars. It does not rise with inflation, so its buying power falls each year. At 2.5% inflation, a fixed payment loses about a fifth of its value in nine years.
Total paid out is the sum of all payouts. It is higher than the balance because the unspent part keeps earning.
Steady returns are assumed. Real investments vary. Poor returns in the first years of withdrawals (sequence-of-returns risk) can shorten how long the money lasts even if the average return is as expected.
Before tax. Payouts from pre-tax accounts are usually taxed as income.
Payout options in real annuity contracts
An insurer's annuity quote is not the same calculation. A life annuity pays for as long as you live, however long that is, so the insurer prices it on life expectancy and its own interest rate. Common options:
Life only: the highest income, ending at death.
Life with period certain: payments for life, but at least for a set number of years (e.g. 10) to your beneficiary.
Joint and survivor: payments continue to a spouse or partner, often at a reduced percentage.
Fixed period: income for a set number of years only. This is the case this calculator models.
Lump-sum or systematic withdrawal: you keep control and draw down yourself.
Use this tool to compare a quote against what the same money could pay if you drew it down yourself over a fixed period.
Country notes
United States: distributions from traditional IRAs and 401(k)s are taxed as ordinary income, and required minimum distributions start at age 73 for most people (see the RMD calculator). The non-taxable part of annuity payments bought with after-tax money is set out in IRS Publication 575 and Publication 939.
Canada: RRSPs must be converted (to a RRIF or annuity) by the end of the year you turn 71, and RRIFs have prescribed minimum withdrawals. Registered annuity payments are taxed as income.
United Kingdom: from a defined-contribution pension you can buy an annuity, use flexi-access drawdown or take lump sums. Usually 25% can be taken tax-free, subject to limits; the rest is taxed as income. MoneyHelper (Pension Wise) offers free guidance for people aged 50 and over.
Tips and common mistakes
Using an accumulation-phase return. During payout the money has a shorter horizon; many people hold more cash and bonds, which earn less.
Ignoring inflation. Plan for a payout that can rise, or lower the return to a "real" rate (return minus inflation) to get a payout in today's money.
Forgetting tax. Enter the after-tax balance, or reduce the payout by your expected tax rate.
Setting the period too short. A 65-year-old has a real chance of living past 90. Running out early is worse than leaving money behind.
Frequently asked questions
What does "level" payout mean?
The same dollar amount every month for the whole period. It does not increase with inflation.
Will the balance be exactly zero at the end?
Yes, if the return you entered is earned every month. With real-world returns the end balance will be higher or lower.
How do I get an inflation-adjusted payout?
Enter a real return (expected return minus expected inflation). The payout is then in today's money, and you would raise it each year by inflation.
Is this the same as an insurer's annuity quote?
No. Insurers price lifetime income on life expectancy, interest rates and their costs. This tool models a fixed-period drawdown at the rate you choose.
Can I see how long a set income will last?
Not yet in the tool. The worked example above shows the formula, and the retirement calculator includes a drawdown view.
Does it include fees?
No. Lower the return by your annual fees.
