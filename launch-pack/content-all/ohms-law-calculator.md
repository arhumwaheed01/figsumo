H1: Ohm's Law Calculator
Ohm's law links the three basic quantities of an electrical circuit: voltage (V), current (I) and resistance (R). Know any two and you can find the third. This calculator solves for whichever value you leave blank and also gives the power the circuit uses.
How to use this calculator
1. Enter exactly two of:
Voltage (V)
Current (A)
Resistance (Ω)
2. Leave the one you want to find blank. By default, voltage is blank, current is 2 A and resistance is 10 Ω.
Results: voltage, current, resistance and power (W).
The formulas
V = I × R
I = V ÷ R
R = V ÷ I
P = V × I = I² × R = V² ÷ R
V: voltage in volts, the electrical "pressure"
I: current in amperes (amps), the flow of charge
R: resistance in ohms (Ω), the opposition to flow
P: power in watts
A handy memory aid is the triangle with V on top and I × R below: cover the one you want to find.
Worked examples
Example 1: the default
I = 2 A, R = 10 Ω → V = 20 V, P = 40 W
Example 2: current through a resistor
12 V across 220 Ω: I = 12 ÷ 220 = 0.0545 A (54.5 mA), dissipating 0.6545 W. A standard ¼ W resistor is fine.
Example 3: an LED resistor
A red LED drops about 2 V and needs 20 mA. From a 5 V supply, the resistor must drop 3 V: R = 3 ÷ 0.020 = 150 Ω (use the next standard value up, 150 Ω), dissipating 0.06 W.
Example 4: a heater
A 120 V heater drawing 12.5 A has a resistance of 120 ÷ 12.5 = 9.6 Ω and uses 1,500 W.
Example 5: a UK kettle
A 3,000 W kettle on 230 V draws 13 A and has a resistance of about V² ÷ P = 17.63 Ω.

Where Ohm's law applies
It holds for resistors and conductors at a steady temperature (ohmic devices). It doesn't directly describe:
LEDs and diodes, whose current rises sharply above a threshold voltage.
Filament bulbs, whose resistance increases as they heat up.
AC circuits with capacitors or motors, where impedance replaces resistance.
For series and parallel combinations, use the resistor calculator; for long cable runs, the voltage drop calculator.
Safety
Household voltages (120 V in North America, 230 V in the UK) can kill. Use these calculations for design and learning; leave mains wiring to a licensed electrician, and follow the NEC (US), the Canadian Electrical Code or BS 7671 (UK).
Tips and common mistakes
Filling in all three boxes; leave one blank.
Forgetting prefixes: 220 kΩ is 220,000 Ω.
Ignoring power ratings: a resistor dissipating more than its rating overheats.
Applying Ohm's law to LEDs without a series resistor.
Notes for US, Canada and UK users
North American outlets supply about 120 V and the UK about 230 V, so the same 1,500 W heater draws 12.5 A in Toronto or Texas and 6.52 A in London. That's one reason UK plugs carry fuses (commonly 3 A or 13 A).
Frequently asked questions
What is Ohm's law?
V = I × R.
How do I calculate current?
Divide voltage by resistance.
How do I calculate resistance?
Divide voltage by current.
How do I calculate power?
Multiply voltage by current.
What unit is resistance measured in?
Ohms (Ω).
Does Ohm's law work for AC?
For purely resistive loads, yes; otherwise use impedance.
