H1: Root Calculator
A root undoes a power. The square root of 16 is 4 because 4² = 16; the cube root of 27 is 3 because 3³ = 27. This calculator finds the nth root of any number: square roots, cube roots and higher, including odd roots of negative numbers.
How to use this calculator
1. Degree n: a positive whole number. 2 gives the square root, 3 the cube root, and so on.
2. Value x: the number to take the root of. Negative values are allowed when n is odd.
Result: the nth root of x, to eight decimal places.
The formula
ⁿ√x = x^(1/n)
For a negative x and odd n, the calculator returns −(|x|^(1/n)), the real root. Even roots of negative numbers have no real value (they're imaginary: √−4 = 2i), so the calculator shows an error.
Worked examples
Example 1: square root of 16
√16 = 4, since 4 × 4 = 16. (−4 is also a square root; the calculator gives the positive, or principal, root.)
Example 2: cube root of −27
∛−27 = −3, because (−3)³ = −27.
Example 3: fifth root of 100
100^(1/5) = 2.51188643
Example 4: average growth rate
An investment doubles in 7 years. The yearly growth factor is the 7th root of 2, so the average annual return is (2^(1/7) − 1) × 100 = 10.41%.
Example 5: music
In equal temperament, each semitone multiplies a note's frequency by the 12th root of 2 = 1.05946309.
Estimating square roots by hand
Find the perfect squares either side. √50 lies between √49 = 7 and √64 = 8, closer to 7. A better estimate: 7 + (50 − 49) ÷ (2 × 7) ≈ 7.07. The exact value is 7.07.
To refine any guess g for √x, average g and x ÷ g (Newton's method): for √50, (7 + 50/7) ÷ 2 = 7.0714.

Simplifying radicals
Pull out perfect-square factors: √72 = √(36 × 2) = 6√2. The prime factorization calculator helps: 72 = 2³ × 3², so pairs of 2 and 3 come out.
Understanding your results
Irrational roots (√2 = 1.41421356…) never end or repeat; the eight decimals shown are rounded.
Tiny rounding errors can appear with floating-point maths: a result such as 2.99999999 for ∛27 means 3.
Rules of roots: √(ab) = √a × √b, and √(a/b) = √a ÷ √b, for non-negative a and b. But √(a + b) is not √a + √b.
Tips and common mistakes
Forgetting the negative square root when solving x² = 16 (x = ±4).
Expecting a real even root of a negative number.
Mixing up roots and division. The cube root of 27 is 3, not 9.
Using n = 1: the first root of x is x itself.
Notes for US, Canada and UK readers
The √ symbol and the words "square root" and "cube root" are used everywhere. US and Canadian texts call √ a "radical" and simplifying √72 to 6√2 "simplifying radicals"; UK GCSE calls the same thing "simplifying surds". Calculator rules for exams differ: some US tests allow only approved models, and UK GCSE has a non-calculator paper, so it's worth practising estimates such as √50 ≈ 7.07 (7.0711).
Frequently asked questions
What is a square root?
A number that, multiplied by itself, gives the original number.
Can I take the cube root of a negative number?
Yes. ∛−8 = −2.
Why can't I take the square root of a negative number?
No real number squared is negative. The answer is imaginary.
How do roots relate to exponents?
The nth root of x equals x to the power 1/n.
What is the square root of 2?
About 1.41421356.
How do I simplify √72?
6√2.
