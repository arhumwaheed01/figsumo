H1: Freelance Rate Calculator
Freelancers and contractors often underprice because they divide the salary they want by 2,080 hours. But you pay your own taxes, benefits and business costs, and you can't bill every working hour. This calculator works backwards from the income you want to the hourly and day rate you need to charge.
How to use this calculator
1. Target income: the annual pay you want for yourself, as you'd think of a salary.
2. Benefits to replace: health insurance, retirement contributions, and the value of paid leave if not covered by time off below.
3. Business expenses: software, equipment, insurance, accountant, coworking, marketing, travel.
4. Tax allowance (%): the share of profit to set aside for income tax and self-employment tax or National Insurance. Use your own figure from a tax calculator.
5. Working weeks: 52 minus holidays, public holidays, sick days and training.
6. Hours per week and utilisation (%): the share of working hours you can actually bill. Admin, sales and gaps between projects mean 50% to 75% is common for many freelancers.
7. Hours per day for a day rate.
Results: revenue needed, billable hours, required hourly rate, day rate and an employee-salary comparison.
The formula
Pre-tax need = Target income + Benefits + Expenses
Revenue needed = Pre-tax need ÷ (1 − Tax allowance)
Billable hours = Working weeks × Hours per week × Utilisation
Hourly rate = Revenue needed ÷ Billable hours
Day rate = Hourly rate × Hours per day
The tax step is a simplification: it treats tax as a flat share of revenue. It's deliberately conservative; your actual tax depends on deductions and rates.
Worked examples
Example 1: a US freelancer
Target income $80,000; benefits $12,000; expenses $9,000 → pre-tax need $101,000.00
Tax allowance 25% → revenue needed $134,666.67
46 working weeks × 40 hours × 65% utilisation = 1,196 billable hours
Hourly rate = $112.60; day rate (8 hours) $900.78
Dividing $80,000 by 2,080 hours gives $38.46 an hour, less than half the rate this freelancer actually needs.
Example 2: a UK contractor
Target £50,000; pension £6,000; expenses £4,000 → £60,000.00
Tax and NI allowance 28% → revenue £83,333.33
44 weeks × 37.5 hours × 70% = 1,155 billable hours
Hourly rate £72.15; day rate (7.5 hours) £541.13
Example 3: a Canadian freelancer
Target $70,000; benefits $8,000; expenses $6,000 → $84,000.00
Tax allowance 30% → revenue $120,000.00
45 weeks × 35 hours × 60% = 945 billable hours
Hourly rate = $126.98; day rate (7 hours) $888.89

Pricing a project
At Example 1's rate (rounded up), a project you estimate at 30 hours should be priced at about $3,390.00, plus a contingency for scope creep.
Retainers
A retainer is a fixed monthly fee for a set number of hours or a defined service. At Example 1's rate, a 20-hour monthly retainer is 20 × $112.60 = $2,252.00. Some freelancers offer a small discount, for example 5% ($2,139.40), in exchange for guaranteed income, which raises utilisation and can justify the lower price. Agree what happens to unused hours (lost, carried forward or refunded) before you start.
Understanding your results
Utilisation is the biggest lever. Going from 65% to 75% billable cuts the hourly rate you need by more than 13%.
Expenses are deductible, so they reduce tax; the flat tax allowance may overstate tax slightly.
Market check: compare the result with what clients pay for similar work. If the market rate is lower, adjust hours, expenses or your target, or specialise.
Value pricing: for some work, pricing by outcome or by project rather than by hour can earn more.
Country notes
United States: self-employed people pay 15.3% self-employment tax on 92.35% of net profit (Social Security part capped at the wage base), plus income tax, usually through quarterly estimated payments. See the self-employment tax calculator.
Canada: self-employed people pay both shares of CPP (11.9% plus CPP2 at 8% on higher earnings in 2026), plus income tax. EI is optional for the self-employed.
United Kingdom: sole traders pay Income Tax and Class 4 National Insurance on profits; contractors working through a limited company need to consider IR35 rules, which can treat them as employees for tax.
Tips and common mistakes
Using 2,080 hours (40 × 52) as billable time.
Forgetting unpaid time: holidays, sick days, admin, sales.
Leaving out the employer-equivalent costs you now pay: pension, health cover, payroll taxes.
Never raising rates. Review them yearly.
Testing your rate
Check the result against the market. Look at rates on freelance platforms and industry surveys for your field and region. If your calculated rate is far above them, cut costs, raise your billable hours or specialise. If it's far below, you may be undercharging.
Frequently asked questions
How do I calculate my freelance hourly rate?
Add your target income, benefits and expenses, gross it up for tax, and divide by realistic billable hours.
What utilisation rate should I use?
Track your own time. Many freelancers bill between half and three-quarters of their working hours.
How do I set a day rate?
Multiply your hourly rate by the hours in a typical day you'd bill.
Should I charge more than my old salary per hour?
Usually much more, because you cover your own costs and unbillable time.
Does this include VAT or sales tax?
No. Add VAT or GST/HST on top if you're registered.
How much should I set aside for tax?
It depends on your country and income; use the relevant tax calculator for a better estimate than a flat percentage.
