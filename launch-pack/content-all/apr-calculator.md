H1: APR Calculator
The annual percentage rate (APR) folds a loan's upfront fees into its interest rate, so two offers with different fees can be compared on one number. Enter the loan amount, the fees you pay up front, the quoted interest rate and the term. The calculator returns the monthly payment and the APR including fees.
How to use this calculator
1. Loan amount: the amount you borrow and repay, as shown on the loan agreement.
2. Upfront fees: charges you pay to get the loan, such as an origination or arrangement fee, discount points or a broker fee. Include only finance charges, not optional products. On a US mortgage, these are listed in Section A of the Loan Estimate.
3. Nominal interest rate: the yearly rate the lender charges on the balance (sometimes called the note rate).
4. Term (years): the full repayment period.
Results: monthly payment, APR (includes fees) and the nominal rate for comparison.
How the APR is calculated
First the monthly payment is worked out on the full loan amount at the nominal rate:
PMT = L × r / (1 − (1 + r)^−n)
L: loan amount
r: nominal rate ÷ 12
n: term in years × 12
Then the calculator treats the fees as money you never really received. It finds the monthly rate a at which the payments repay only the net amount:
L − Fees = PMT × (1 − (1 + a)^−n) / a
APR = a × 12
There is no algebraic solution for a, so the tool finds it numerically by bisection and displays the APR to two decimal places (the examples below show three). Payments are monthly and made at the end of each month.
Worked examples
Example 1: $20,000 personal loan, $500 fee, 6.5% for 5 years
PMT = 20,000 at 6.5% over 60 months = $391.32
Net amount received: 20,000 − 500 = $19,500
The rate that links $19,500 to 60 payments of $391.32 gives APR = 7.563%
Total repaid: $23,479.38; total cost of credit including the fee: $3,979.38
A $500 fee adds about one percentage point to a 5-year loan of this size. A competing offer at 7.2% with no fee has an APR of 7.2%, so it is the cheaper loan per dollar you actually receive, even though its payment ($397.91) is higher because you get the full $20,000.
Example 2: $300,000 mortgage, $6,000 in fees, 6.25% for 30 years
PMT = $1,847.15 a month
Net received: $294,000
APR = 6.442% if the loan runs the full 30 years
APR spreads the fee over the whole term. If you sell or refinance after 5 years, the fee is spread over only 60 payments and the effective rate for that period is about 6.736%. That is why a loan with high fees and a low rate suits someone who keeps it a long time, and a no-fee loan suits someone who may move soon.
Understanding your results
APR above the nominal rate means fees are included. The bigger the fees and the shorter the term, the bigger the gap.
APR equals the nominal rate when fees are zero.
APR is not the same as the effective annual rate. APR here is the monthly rate × 12. Compounded monthly, the Example 1 loan has an effective annual rate of 7.83%.
A lender's official APR may differ. In the US, Regulation Z (Truth in Lending) defines which charges count as finance charges, and it varies by loan type. Use the APR on your Loan Estimate or credit agreement for formal comparisons, and this tool to test "what if" scenarios.
Limits of APR
It assumes you keep the loan to the end of the term, which many borrowers don't.
On adjustable-rate loans, the APR is based on assumed future rates, so it is less reliable.
It ignores costs that are not finance charges (for example, title insurance on some loans), even though you pay them.
It doesn't compare loans of different lengths fairly: a longer loan can have a lower APR but a much higher total interest cost. Check the total repaid as well.
Country notes
United States: the Truth in Lending Act and Regulation Z require lenders to disclose APR. The CFPB's Loan Estimate shows it on page 3 for mortgages.
Canada: federally regulated lenders must disclose the annual cost of borrowing, which includes most non-interest charges. Provincial rules cover other lenders.
United Kingdom: credit adverts and agreements must show the APR under the Consumer Credit (Disclosure of Information) rules; mortgage documents show an APRC (annual percentage rate of charge). A "representative APR" only has to be offered to at least 51% of successful applicants, so your own rate can be higher.
Tips and common mistakes
Including the fee in the loan amount twice. If the fee is added to the loan, enter the higher loan amount and the fee; if it is deducted from what you receive, enter the original amount and the fee.
Leaving out discount points. Points are prepaid interest and belong in the fee field.
Comparing APRs on different terms. Compare like-for-like terms, then look at total cost.
Ignoring how long you will keep the loan. Re-run the numbers with the term you expect to keep it.
Frequently asked questions
Why is the APR higher than my interest rate?
Because the fees are treated as extra cost of borrowing. You pay interest on the full amount but receive less, which raises the true rate.
Which fees should I include?
Charges you must pay to get the credit: origination or arrangement fees, points, mortgage broker fees and similar lender charges. Leave out costs you'd pay anyway, such as a home inspection.
Is a lower APR always better?
Not always. A longer loan may show a lower APR but cost more in total interest, and a high-fee loan only delivers its APR if you keep it for the full term.
What is the difference between APR and APY?
APR is a simple yearly rate (monthly rate × 12) used for loans. APY or AER includes compounding and is used for savings. For a loan, the effective annual rate is the APY-style equivalent.
Does the calculator handle fees added to the loan?
Yes, as long as you enter the loan amount including the financed fee and the fee itself. The APR is computed on what you actually receive.
Why does my lender's APR differ slightly?
Lenders follow legal rules on which charges are finance charges and how to handle irregular first periods. Small differences are normal.
