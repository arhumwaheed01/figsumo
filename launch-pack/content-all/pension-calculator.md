H1: Pension Calculator (Defined Benefit)
This calculator estimates the yearly and monthly income from a defined benefit (DB) pension: the kind of workplace pension that pays a set income based on your salary and years of service, rather than on investment returns. Enter your years of service, your final average salary and the plan's benefit factor (accrual rate) to get the estimated annual and monthly pension.
How to use this calculator
1. Years of service: the credited years (or years and months as a decimal) in the plan. Your annual pension statement shows your credited service.
2. Final average salary: the pensionable pay the plan uses, often an average of your highest or final 3 to 5 years. Check the plan's definition; some plans exclude overtime or bonuses.
3. Benefit factor (% per year): the percentage of salary earned for each year of service, from the plan booklet. For example 2% per year, or 1/60 (1.667%) or 1/80 (1.25%) in many UK schemes.
Results: estimated annual pension and estimated monthly pension, before tax.
The formula
Annual pension = Benefit factor × Final average salary × Years of service
Monthly pension = Annual pension ÷ 12
For example, 2% × 25 years = 50% of final average salary.
Worked examples
Example 1: 25 years, $85,000 final average salary, 2% factor
Annual pension = 0.02 × 85,000 × 25 = $42,500.00
Monthly pension = $3,541.67
Example 2: a 1/60th scheme with 30 years and £62,000 pensionable pay
Annual pension = 30 ÷ 60 × 62,000 = £31,000.00, or £2,583.33 a month. The calculator shows a $ sign, but the arithmetic is the same in pounds.
Enter the factor as 1.6667 to reproduce this in the calculator.
Retiring early
Many plans cut the pension for each year you retire before normal retirement age. If Example 1's plan reduced benefits by 5% a year and you retired 3 years early, the pension would fall to $36,125.00 a year ($3,010.42 a month). Reductions differ widely; check your plan's early retirement table.
Understanding your results
The figure is before tax. DB pensions are taxed as income in the US, Canada and the UK.
Inflation: if the pension isn't indexed, its buying power falls. After 15 years of 2.5% inflation, $42,500.00 buys what $29,344.79 buys today. Many public plans index pensions; many private plans don't, or cap increases.
Normal retirement age matters. The formula gives the unreduced pension at the plan's normal age.
Survivor options: choosing a joint-and-survivor pension usually reduces your own payment.
Lump sum or monthly pension?
Some plans offer a one-time lump sum instead of the monthly pension. One way to compare: the present value of the pension. Paid for 25 years and discounted at 5%, Example 1's $42,500.00 a year is worth about $598,992.64 today. A lump-sum offer well below that is poor value at that discount rate; one above it may be attractive, especially if you're in poor health or want control. Living longer than expected favors the monthly pension, which is a form of longevity insurance. The annuity payout calculator shows what a lump sum could pay if you drew it down yourself.
Country notes
United States: private DB plans are covered by ERISA, and the Pension Benefit Guaranty Corporation (PBGC) insures benefits up to legal limits if a plan fails. Federal employees under FERS earn 1% per year (1.1% if retiring at 62 or later with 20 years); for example, 1.1% × $95,000 × 30 years = $31,350.00 [VERIFY with OPM]. Social Security is separate; see the Social Security calculator.
Canada: many public-sector plans use about 2% per year, often integrated with CPP (a lower rate on earnings up to the YMPE). CPP and OAS are paid on top; see the CPP calculator. Plans are regulated federally or provincially.
United Kingdom: DB schemes (final salary or career average, CARE) commonly use accrual rates such as 1/60 or 1/80. The Pension Protection Fund protects members of eligible schemes if the employer becomes insolvent. Most workers today are in defined contribution schemes instead; see the workplace pension calculator. The State Pension is separate.
Defined benefit vs defined contribution
DB: the employer promises an income; investment risk sits with the plan.
DC (401(k), RRSP group plans, UK workplace pensions): your pot depends on contributions and returns; you carry the risk. Use the retirement calculator for DC savings.
Tips and common mistakes
Using current salary instead of final average salary. Plans average over a set period.
Ignoring integration with government pensions. Some plans reduce benefits to account for Social Security or CPP.
Forgetting early retirement reductions. They can be large.
Assuming indexation. Check whether and how the pension rises each year.
Frequently asked questions
How is a defined benefit pension calculated?
Usually as accrual rate × pensionable salary × years of service, with adjustments for early retirement and survivor options.
What is a typical accrual rate?
It varies; 1% to 2% per year is common in North America, and 1/60 to 1/80 in UK schemes.
Is my pension taxed?
Yes, as income in most cases. Some UK schemes allow a tax-free lump sum by exchanging part of the pension.
Should I take the lump sum?
It depends on the offer, your health, other income and how comfortable you are managing investments. Compare it with the present value of the pension and get advice.
What happens to my pension if my employer goes bust?
In the US, the PBGC insures private DB benefits up to limits; in the UK, the Pension Protection Fund covers eligible schemes; in Canada, protection varies by province (Ontario has the PBGF).
Does the calculator include inflation increases?
No. It gives the starting pension. Increases depend on the plan's rules.
