H1: Future Value Calculator
The future value of a sum of money is what it grows to after a number of years at a given interest rate, once compound interest has been added. Enter the amount you have today, the annual rate, the number of years and how often interest compounds. The calculator returns the future value.
How to use this calculator
1. Present value: the lump sum you start with, e.g. a deposit or an investment you make today.
2. Annual rate: the nominal yearly interest rate or expected return, as a percentage.
3. Years: how long the money grows.
4. Compounds per year: how often interest is added: 1 (annually), 2 (semi-annually), 4 (quarterly), 12 (monthly) or 365 (daily). Your account terms say which applies.
Result: future value.
The formula
FV = PV × (1 + r ÷ m)^(m × t)
FV: future value
PV: present value
r: annual rate as a decimal
m: compounding periods per year
t: years
The growth (interest earned) is FV − PV. If interest compounded continuously, the limit of this formula would be FV = PV × e^(r × t).
Worked examples
Example 1: $10,000 at 7% for 10 years, compounded monthly
FV = 10,000 × (1 + 0.07 ÷ 12)^(12 × 10) = $20,096.61
Growth: $10,096.61

Example 2: $25,000 at 4.5% for 18 years, compounded quarterly
FV = 25,000 × (1 + 0.045 ÷ 4)^(4 × 18) = $55,944.13
If inflation averages 2.5% a year over the same period, that sum is worth about $35,869.47 in today's money (divide by 1.025^18).
Understanding your results
Future value is in future dollars. It doesn't account for inflation, taxes or fees.
It assumes a constant rate. Savings rates change; investment returns vary year to year. For investments, treat the rate as an assumption and test a range.
Doubling time: the "rule of 72" estimates years to double as 72 ÷ rate. At 7%, that is about 10.3 years; the exact figure with annual compounding is ln 2 ÷ ln 1.07 = 10.24 years.
How long to reach a target
You can also rearrange the formula to find the time needed:
t = ln(FV ÷ PV) ÷ (m × ln(1 + r ÷ m))
Growing $10,000 to $25,000 at 7% compounded monthly takes ln(2.5) ÷ (12 × ln(1 + 0.07 ÷ 12)) = 13.1 years. At 5% it would take 18.4 years. This is a quick way to check whether a goal is realistic before committing to a plan.
Uses in everyday planning
Savings goals: how much a deposit you make today will be worth when a child starts school or when you plan to retire.
Comparing accounts: run two rates and compounding frequencies side by side to see the difference in money, not percentages.
Cost of waiting: money you invest 5 years later has 5 fewer years to compound; compare the two future values to see what delay costs.
Future value vs present value
Future value moves money forward in time; present value moves it back. They are the same equation rearranged: PV = FV ÷ (1 + r ÷ m)^(m × t). Use the present value calculator when you know the amount you need later and want to know what it's worth today.
Adding regular deposits
This calculator handles a single lump sum. If you also add money every month, the future value of those deposits is calculated with the annuity formula, FV = PMT × ((1 + i)^n − 1) ÷ i, and added to the lump-sum result. The compound interest calculator and annuity calculator do this for you.
Country notes
United States: savings rates are quoted as APY, which already includes compounding. If you enter an APY, set compounds per year to 1 so you don't count compounding twice.
Canada: banks quote GIC and savings rates as annual rates with a stated compounding frequency; for registered accounts (TFSA, RRSP) growth isn't taxed each year.
United Kingdom: savings rates are quoted as AER, which works like APY. With an AER, use 1 compounding period per year.
Tips and common mistakes
Double-counting compounding. APY and AER already include compounding; enter them with 1 period per year.
Using percentages as decimals. Enter 7 for 7%.
Ignoring tax. Interest in a taxable account is taxed each year, which lowers the effective growth rate.
Forgetting inflation. A large future number can buy less than you expect.
Over-optimistic returns. For long horizons, small rate changes have large effects; test several rates.
Quick check
Rerun Example 1 with a 5% rate to see how sensitive the result is to the return you assume.
Frequently asked questions
What is future value?
The value of money at a future date after interest or investment growth is added.
How does compounding frequency affect future value?
More frequent compounding adds interest on interest sooner, so the future value is slightly higher at the same nominal rate.
What is the difference between simple and compound interest?
Simple interest is earned only on the original amount; compound interest is also earned on interest already added. See the simple interest calculator.
Can I calculate future value with monthly contributions?
Not in this tool yet. Use the compound interest calculator, or add the annuity formula result yourself.
What rate should I use for investments?
There's no guaranteed figure. Many people test a cautious and an optimistic rate and plan around the cautious one.
How do I find the rate needed to reach a target?
Rearrange: r = m × ((FV ÷ PV)^(1 ÷ (m × t)) − 1). For example, doubling in 10 years with annual compounding needs about 7.18%.
