H1: Budget Calculator
This budget calculator shows whether your monthly take-home pay covers your main spending and a savings goal, and how much is left over (or missing) at the end of the month. Enter your income and five spending lines. It totals what you have planned to spend and save, gives the monthly surplus or deficit, and works out your planned savings rate.
How to use this calculator
1. Monthly income: your take-home pay after tax and payroll deductions, plus any regular income such as benefits or a side job. Use your payslip's net pay, or the paycheck calculator if you only know your salary. If you're paid weekly, multiply weekly pay by 52 and divide by 12; don't just multiply by 4.
2. Housing: rent or mortgage payment, plus property tax, home insurance, service charges and utilities if you want them included here.
3. Transportation: car payment, insurance, fuel, parking, transit passes.
4. Food: groceries plus eating out.
5. Other expenses: everything else, such as phone, internet, subscriptions, childcare, debt payments, clothing, health costs, gifts.
6. Savings goal: the amount you want to put aside each month, including retirement contributions you make from take-home pay.
Results: total planned outflows, monthly surplus / deficit and savings rate.
The formula
Total planned outflows = Housing + Transportation + Food + Other + Savings goal
Surplus (or deficit)   = Monthly income − Total planned outflows
Savings rate           = Savings goal ÷ Monthly income × 100
A positive surplus is unallocated money. A negative figure (a deficit) means the plan spends and saves more than you earn. The savings rate counts only the savings goal you entered, not the surplus.
Worked examples
Example 1: income $6,500
Housing $1,800, transportation $500, food $700, other $900, savings $500.
Outflows = 1,800 + 500 + 700 + 900 + 500 = $4,400.00
Surplus = 6,500 − 4,400 = $2,100.00
Savings rate = 500 ÷ 6,500 = 7.7%
The plan leaves a large unallocated surplus. If all of it were saved too, the savings rate would be 40.0%. In practice, a surplus this large usually means some spending is missing from the plan, so check your bank statements before counting on it.
Example 2: income $3,900
Housing $1,450, transportation $380, food $520, other $650, savings $300.
Outflows = $3,300.00; surplus $600.00
Savings rate 7.7%; housing takes 37.2% of income
Housing above about 30% of income is a common warning sign; it leaves less room for everything else.
Understanding your results
Surplus: money with no job yet. Give it one (extra savings, debt payments, a sinking fund for irregular bills) or it tends to get spent.
Deficit: the plan doesn't balance. Cut a line, raise income, or lower the savings goal temporarily. Don't cover a deficit with credit cards; interest makes the gap grow.
Savings rate: the share of take-home pay you plan to save. Higher rates build an emergency fund and retirement savings faster.
A simple framework: 50/30/20
One popular starting point splits take-home pay into 50% needs (housing, basic food, transport, minimum debt payments), 30% wants and 20% savings and extra debt repayment. On $3,900 that is $1,950.00 for needs, $1,170.00 for wants and $780.00 for saving. It is a guide, not a rule: in high-cost cities, housing alone can exceed 50%.
Building a budget that holds up
Start from real spending. Download two or three months of bank and card statements and sort them into the five lines.
Spread irregular costs. Divide annual bills (car insurance, holidays, gifts, subscriptions) by 12 and include them in "Other".
Pay yourself first. Set up an automatic transfer for the savings goal on payday.
Build an emergency fund. A common target is three to six months of essential spending. Use the savings calculator to see how long it will take.
Tackle expensive debt. If "Other" includes card payments, the debt payoff calculator shows how much faster extra payments clear it.
Review monthly. Budgets fail when they stop matching reality.
Country notes
United States: the CFPB publishes free budgeting worksheets. Employer 401(k) contributions are taken before take-home pay, so don't count them twice in the savings line.
Canada: the Financial Consumer Agency of Canada offers a free budget planner. TFSA and RRSP contributions made from your bank account belong in the savings line.
United Kingdom: MoneyHelper has a free budget planner. Council Tax belongs in housing; workplace pension contributions are already deducted from net pay.
Tips and common mistakes
Using gross salary. Budget from take-home pay.
Forgetting small recurring charges. Subscriptions add up quickly.
Leaving no slack. Budget a small "miscellaneous" amount in Other so surprises don't break the plan.
Being too strict. A budget with no room for wants is hard to keep.
Frequently asked questions
Should I use gross or net income?
Net (take-home) income. Taxes and payroll deductions are taken before you can spend the money.
What counts as housing?
Rent or mortgage at minimum. Many people also include property tax, insurance, utilities and service charges so the line shows the full cost of the home.
How much should I save each month?
There is no single answer. Common guides suggest about 20% of take-home pay, but start with what you can sustain and build up.
What if I have a deficit?
Look first at the largest flexible lines (food, transportation, other), then consider whether housing is sustainable. Avoid filling the gap with high-interest borrowing.
Does the savings rate include my surplus?
No. It uses only the savings goal you entered. Move the surplus into the savings line if you plan to save it.
How do I handle irregular income?
Budget on a cautious month's income, and put anything above it into savings or a buffer account.
