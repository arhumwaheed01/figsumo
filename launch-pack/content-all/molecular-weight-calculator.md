H1: Molecular Weight Calculator
Molecular weight (molar mass) is the mass of one mole of a substance, in grams per mole. It's the bridge between grams, which you weigh, and moles, which chemical equations count. This calculator finds the molar mass from a measured sample: enter its mass and the number of moles it contains.
How to use this calculator
1. Sample mass (g): default 18.
2. Amount (mol): default 1.
Result: molecular weight in g/mol.
To find the molar mass from a chemical formula instead, add up the atomic weights, as shown below.
The formulas
From a sample:
M = mass (g) ÷ amount (mol)
From a formula:
M = Σ (number of atoms of each element × atomic weight)

Worked examples
Example 1: the default
18 g ÷ 1 mol = 18 g/mol, close to water.
Example 2: water from its formula
H₂O = 2 × 1.008 + 15.999 = 18.015 g/mol
Example 3: glucose
C₆H₁₂O₆ = 6 × 12.011 + 12 × 1.008 + 6 × 15.999 = 180.156 g/mol. Carbon makes up 40.00% of its mass.
Example 4: brackets
Ca(OH)₂ = 40.078 + 2 × (15.999 + 1.008) = 74.092 g/mol. Multiply everything inside the brackets by the subscript.
Example 5: identifying an unknown
0.100 mol of an unknown gas has a mass of 4.40 g: 4.40 ÷ 0.100 = 44.0 g/mol, which matches carbon dioxide (CO₂ = 44.009 g/mol).
Example 6: sulfuric acid
H₂SO₄ = 98.072 g/mol.
Terminology
Molar mass (g/mol): mass of one mole; what this calculator gives.
Molecular weight / relative molecular mass (Mr): the same number without units, relative to 1/12 of a carbon-12 atom.
Molecular mass (Da or u): mass of a single molecule; numerically equal to molar mass.
Formula weight: used for ionic compounds like NaCl (58.44 g/mol) that don't form discrete molecules.
One mole contains 6.02214076 × 10²³ particles (the Avogadro constant, exact since 2019).
Understanding your results
Hydrates include their water: CuSO₄·5H₂O is 249.69 g/mol.
Precision depends on your mass and mole measurements; weigh carefully.
To turn molar mass into concentration, use the molarity calculator.
Tips and common mistakes
Forgetting subscripts outside brackets.
Using mass numbers (O = 16) when atomic weights are needed for precise work.
Leaving out water of crystallisation.
Confusing grams and moles in the inputs.
Notes for US, Canada and UK users
Spelling: US textbooks write "sulfur"; UK schools now use "sulfur" too, following IUPAC, though older British books say "sulphur". Canadian texts generally use "sulfur". Enter the symbol S either way.
Terminology: US and Canadian high-school chemistry often says "molar mass" (g/mol). UK GCSE and A-level use "relative formula mass" (Mr, no units) and "relative molecular mass" for molecules. The numbers are the same: water is 18.02 g/mol, or Mr = 18.
Rounding in exams: UK exam boards usually give relative atomic masses to one decimal place (Cl = 35.5) in their data sheets, so your answer may differ slightly from the calculator's, which uses more precise values. Use the values your exam provides.
Frequently asked questions
What is molecular weight?
The mass of one mole of a substance, in g/mol.
How do I calculate molar mass from a formula?
Multiply each element's atomic weight by its number of atoms and add them.
What is the molar mass of water?
About 18.015 g/mol.
Is molar mass the same as molecular weight?
Numerically yes; molar mass has units of g/mol.
What is Avogadro's number?
6.02214076 × 10²³ per mole.
Can I type a chemical formula?
Not yet; add atomic weights as shown above.
