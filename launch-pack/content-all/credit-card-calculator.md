H1: Credit Card Calculator
This calculator shows how long it takes to clear a credit card balance if you only make the minimum payment each month, and how much interest that costs. Enter the balance, the card's APR and how your issuer sets the minimum payment. The answer is usually a surprise: minimum payments are designed to keep the account in good standing, not to pay the debt off quickly.
How to use this calculator
1. Balance: the amount you owe today, from your latest statement or app.
2. APR: the purchase APR on the statement. If part of the balance is on a different rate (cash advance, promotional rate), use a weighted average or run each part separately.
3. Minimum payment (% of balance): the percentage of the balance your issuer requires each month. The terms and conditions or the statement's "minimum payment" section explain the formula.
4. Minimum payment floor: the lowest minimum the issuer will accept, such as $25 or $35, used when the percentage gives a smaller figure.
Results: months to pay off (min payments), total interest paid and total amount paid. No new purchases are assumed.
The formula
The calculator simulates the card month by month:
Interest this month = Balance × APR ÷ 12
Payment = max(Floor, Balance × Min %)   (never more than balance + interest)
New balance = Balance + Interest − Payment
It repeats until the balance is cleared or 600 months (50 years) have passed. Because the payment is a percentage of a shrinking balance, the payment shrinks too, which is why minimum-only payoff takes so long. When the percentage gets small, the floor takes over and finishes the debt.
Card issuers charge interest on the average daily balance, at a daily periodic rate (APR ÷ 365 or 360). The monthly approximation used here is close for a balance that isn't changing during the month.
Worked examples
Example 1: $5,000 at 21.99%, minimum 3% of balance, $35 floor
First month: interest = 5,000 × 0.2199 ÷ 12 = $91.62; minimum = max($35, 3% × 5,000) = $150.00
Months to pay off: 176 (about 14.7 years)
Total interest: $6,669.10; total paid $11,669.10
Example 2: the same card with a fixed $200 a month
Paying a steady $200 instead of the falling minimum clears the balance in 34 months with $1,748.69 of interest, a saving of $4,920.41. (Use the credit card payoff calculator to test fixed payments and payoff dates.)
When the minimum barely covers interest
With a 2% minimum and a $25 floor at 21.99%, the payment is only slightly larger than the interest for years. After the 50-year limit, $1,828.68 would still be owed, after $34,695.17 of interest. If your card's minimum works like this, paying only the minimum is not a realistic plan.
Understanding your results
Months to pay off assumes you never use the card again and the APR doesn't change.
Total interest paid is the cost of carrying the balance. Compare it with the original balance.
Total amount paid is everything you pay over the period.
If the result is 600 months, the debt is not cleared within 50 years with these settings (see the note above).
How issuers set minimum payments
Common formulas:
a fixed percentage of the balance (as modelled here), often 2–3%;
interest and fees for the month plus 1% of the balance;
a fixed floor such as $25–$35, or the whole balance if it is below the floor.
The second formula pays off much faster than a plain 2% because the 1% always reduces principal. Check your cardholder agreement to see which applies.
Ways to pay off faster
Pay a fixed amount, not the falling minimum. Keeping the payment at the first month's minimum cuts years off.
Pay more than once a month. Interest is charged on the daily balance, so earlier payments save interest.
Move the balance to a lower rate. A balance transfer card or a debt consolidation loan can help if the fees are low and you stop adding new debt.
Prioritize the highest APR if you have several cards (the avalanche method), or the smallest balance for motivation (the snowball method). The debt payoff calculator helps compare.
Ask the issuer for a lower rate. It costs nothing to ask, especially with a good payment record.
Country notes
United States: the CARD Act requires statements to show how long minimum-only payment would take and the cost, and what payment would clear the balance in 3 years. Most issuers offer a grace period on purchases if you pay the full statement balance by the due date.
Canada: the minimum payment for credit cards issued by federally regulated institutions must include interest and fees plus at least 1% of the balance [VERIFY current FCAC rule]. Quebec sets a higher minimum by law, rising to 5% of the balance [VERIFY phase-in schedule with the Office de la protection du consommateur].
United Kingdom: the minimum must cover at least interest, fees and 1% of the balance. Under FCA persistent debt rules, if you have paid more in interest and charges than principal over 18 months, your provider must contact you, and after 36 months offer a way to clear the balance faster. Card APRs are usually quoted as a representative APR.
Tips and common mistakes
Using the balance transfer rate as the APR. Use the rate that applies after any promotion ends.
Forgetting new spending. Every new purchase adds to the time and cost.
Ignoring fees. Late fees and over-limit fees make payoff slower.
Assuming the minimum is safe. It keeps you in good standing but is the most expensive way to repay.
Frequently asked questions
Why does paying the minimum take so long?
Because most of a small payment goes to interest, and on percentage-based minimums the payment falls as the balance falls, so principal shrinks very slowly.
How is credit card interest calculated?
Issuers apply a daily rate (APR ÷ 365 or 360) to the balance each day and add it up for the billing cycle. At 21.99%, the daily rate is about 0.0602%; on an average daily balance of $2,400 over 30 days, that is about $43.38 of interest.
Does this include new purchases?
No. It assumes the card is not used while you repay.
What does a 600-month result mean?
That the balance is not cleared within 50 years at these settings. Increase the payment percentage or floor, or use a fixed payment instead.
Will paying the minimum hurt my credit score?
Paying on time helps your payment history, but a high balance relative to your limit (utilization) can weigh on scores. Paying the balance down improves utilization.
Is a balance transfer worth it?
It can be, if the transfer fee is low, the promotional period is long enough, and you don't add new debt. Compare the fee with the interest you would save.
