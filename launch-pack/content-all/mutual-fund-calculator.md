H1: Mutual Fund Calculator
This calculator projects the value of a mutual fund (or ETF) investment, with a starting amount and regular monthly additions, and shows how much the fund's annual expenses cost you over time. Enter the initial investment, the monthly amount, the number of years, the gross return you expect and the fund's expense ratio.
How to use this calculator
1. Initial investment: the lump sum you invest at the start.
2. Monthly investment: the amount you add each month, for example by automatic investment.
3. Years: how long you'll keep investing.
4. Gross annual return: the return you assume before fund costs. Use a cautious long-run figure; returns are never guaranteed.
5. Expense ratio: the fund's ongoing annual charge as a percentage, from its prospectus or fact sheet (called the management expense ratio, MER, in Canada and the ongoing charges figure, OCF, in the UK).
Results: future value after expenses, future value before expenses and cost of expenses (estimate).
The formula
Net return = Gross return − Expense ratio
FV = P × (1 + i)^n + M × ((1 + i)^n − 1) ÷ i
P: initial investment; M: monthly investment
i: monthly rate = net annual return ÷ 12; n: years × 12
Monthly contributions are added at the end of each month, and growth compounds monthly.
The "before expenses" figure uses the gross return. The difference between the two is the estimated cost of fees, including the growth those fees would have earned.

Understanding your results
After expenses is the more realistic projection, but still before tax and before any sales loads or advisory fees.
Cost of expenses shows the long-run price of the fund's ongoing charge.
Returns vary. A real fund's yearly returns swing up and down; this projection assumes a smooth average. See the average return calculator for why averages can mislead.
Other fund costs
Sales loads: some funds charge a front-end load on purchases. A 5.75% load on $10,000 means only $9,425.00 is invested. At 6.5% for 10 years, that grows to $18,022.33, versus $19,121.84 without the load.
12b-1 fees (US) are part of the expense ratio; trailing commissions (Canada) are part of the MER.
Platform and advisory fees are charged separately by many brokers and advisers. Add them to the expense ratio for a full picture.
Trading costs inside the fund are not in the expense ratio but still reduce returns.
Choosing between funds with this calculator
Run the calculator once for each fund you are considering, keeping the gross return the same and changing only the expense ratio. The difference in the after-expenses value is the price of choosing the more expensive fund. Then ask whether that fund offers something worth the cost, such as a strategy you can't get cheaply elsewhere. Broad index funds and ETFs tracking the same market are close substitutes, so their costs are the main difference between them. For actively managed funds, compare the fee with the fund's long-run record after costs against a suitable index, keeping in mind that past outperformance often doesn't last.
Country notes
United States: fund costs appear in the prospectus fee table. The SEC's Mutual Fund Cost Calculator (via FINRA's Fund Analyzer) lets you compare funds. Funds pay out taxable distributions in taxable accounts; holding them in a 401(k) or IRA defers tax.
Canada: the fund facts document lists the MER and any trailing commission. Since 2022, funds can't charge deferred sales charges (DSC) on new purchases. Hold funds in a TFSA or RRSP to shelter growth.
United Kingdom: the key information document shows the ongoing charges figure; platforms add their own fees. A stocks and shares ISA (£20,000 a year allowance) or a pension shelters growth from tax.
Tips and common mistakes
Using recent returns as the assumption. A fund's best five years are not a forecast.
Ignoring all-in costs. Add platform or advice fees to the expense ratio.
Comparing funds on past returns alone. Costs are one of the few things you know in advance.
Stopping contributions in downturns. Regular investing buys more units when prices are low (dollar-cost averaging).
Frequently asked questions
What is an expense ratio?
The percentage of a fund's assets taken each year to pay for management and running costs. It is deducted inside the fund, so you don't see a separate bill.
How much does a 1% fee cost over time?
More than it sounds: it compounds. Example 2 shows tens of thousands of dollars on a modest plan over 30 years.
What return should I assume?
There's no reliable figure. Many people test several rates, such as 4%, 6% and 8%, and plan around the lower ones.
Is a mutual fund different from an ETF?
Both pool money into a portfolio. ETFs trade on an exchange during the day and often have lower costs; mutual funds trade once a day at the closing price.
Does the calculator include taxes?
No. Taxes on distributions and gains depend on the account type and your country.
Why is the "before expenses" figure higher?
Because it assumes the fund's costs were zero. The difference is what the expense ratio costs you.
