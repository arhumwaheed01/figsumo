H1: Army Body Fat Calculator
The U.S. Army estimates body fat with a tape measure rather than scales or scans, so soldiers can be assessed quickly anywhere. This calculator applies the Department of Defense circumference equations, which use height and neck and waist measurements (plus hips for women), to estimate body fat percentage. It's also a useful home estimate for anyone, as long as you understand its limits.
How to use this calculator
1. Sex: the equations differ for men and women.
2. Units: imperial (inches) or metric (centimetres).
3. Height: without shoes.
4. Neck: measured just below the larynx (Adam's apple), with the tape sloping slightly down to the front.
5. Abdomen / waist: for men, at the navel; for women, at the narrowest point of the natural waist (as in the two-site method).
6. Hip (women only): at the widest point of the buttocks.
Measure on bare skin, standing relaxed, without pulling the tape tight; take each measurement three times and average them.
Results: body fat percentage with a note that official screening uses age-specific maximum standards.
The formulas (two-site DoD circumference method)
All measurements in inches:
Men:   %BF = 86.010 × log10(waist − neck) − 70.041 × log10(height) + 36.76
Women: %BF = 163.205 × log10(waist + hip − neck) − 97.684 × log10(height) − 78.387
These are the Hodgdon and Beckett equations developed for the U.S. Navy and used across the DoD. Metric inputs are converted to inches first (175 cm = 68.9 in).
Note on the current Army method: in 2023 the Army adopted a one-site tape test that uses abdominal circumference and body weight [VERIFY current regulation]. This page's calculator currently implements the two-site method; the one-site method is being added.
Worked examples
Example 1: man, 69 in tall, neck 15 in, waist 34 in
waist − neck = 19; log10(19) = 1.2788
log10(69) = 1.8388
%BF = 86.010 × 1.2788 − 70.041 × 1.8388 + 36.76 = 18.0%
If his waist were 36 in, the estimate would rise to 21.7%. The waist measurement drives most of the change.
Example 2: woman, 64 in tall, neck 13 in, waist 30 in, hip 38 in
waist + hip − neck = 55
%BF = 163.205 × log10(55) − 97.684 × log10(64) − 78.387 = 29.2%
Example 3: one-site method (for comparison)
Using the one-site male equation [VERIFY coefficients] for a 180 lb man with a 34 in abdomen: −26.97 − 0.12 × 180 + 1.99 × 34 = 19.1%. Different equations can give somewhat different results for the same person.
Example 4: metric measurements
A man 180 cm tall with a 40 cm neck and a 90 cm waist:
In inches: height 70.87, neck 15.75, waist 35.43
%BF = 86.010 × log10(19.69) − 70.041 × log10(70.87) + 36.76 = 18.5%
The widely quoted metric version of the Navy equation, 495 ÷ (1.0324 − 0.19077 × log10(waist − neck) + 0.15456 × log10(height)) − 450, gives 18.4% for the same person. The small difference comes from how the two versions were fitted, so use one version consistently when tracking change.
Example 5: woman, metric
160 cm tall, neck 32 cm, waist 74 cm, hip 98 cm. In inches: 62.99, 12.60, 29.13 and 38.58. Waist + hip − neck = 55.12 in, giving 30.0%.
Understanding your results
It's an estimate. Circumference equations typically differ from laboratory methods such as DEXA by a few percentage points for individuals, more for very muscular or very lean people.
Consistency matters more than precision. Use the same tape, time of day and technique to track change.
Army standards set maximum body fat percentages that vary by age group and sex. Soldiers who exceed the screening table weight for their height are taped; those over the allowable body fat may be enrolled in a body composition program. Check the current regulation for the limits that apply [VERIFY current AR 600-9 tables].
General health ranges: for non-military use, see the body fat calculator, which explains ranges and other methods.
Measurement tips
Use a non-stretch tape, read to the nearest half inch (or nearest 0.5 cm).
Don't suck in your stomach or flex your neck.
Measure the neck below the larynx, the male abdomen at the navel, and the female waist at its narrowest point.
Repeat until three measurements agree within a quarter inch.
Tips and common mistakes
Mixing units: entering centimetres while inches is selected.
Measuring over clothing.
Comparing results from different equations as if they were identical.
Treating the result as a diagnosis. It's a field estimate of body composition.
Notes for Canada and UK users
The equations here are US Department of Defense standards, and the age-group limits belong to US Army regulations. The Canadian Armed Forces and the UK Armed Forces set their own fitness and body-composition rules, so US limits don't apply to them [VERIFY current CAF and MOD policies]. Civilians in any country can still use the tape method as a free home estimate, as long as they measure the same way each time.
Frequently asked questions
How does the Army calculate body fat?
With a tape-measure method: historically the two-site neck and waist (plus hip for women) equations, and since 2023 a one-site abdominal method [VERIFY].
Is the Army tape test accurate?
Reasonably for groups, less precise for individuals. It can underestimate or overestimate by several percentage points.
Where do I measure my waist for the tape test?
Men at the navel; women at the narrowest point between the ribs and hips for the two-site method.
What is the maximum body fat for the Army?
It depends on age and sex; check the current Army regulation.
Can I use this if I'm not in the military?
Yes, as a convenient home estimate.
Why does my neck measurement matter?
A larger neck relative to the waist suggests more lean mass, so it lowers the estimate.
