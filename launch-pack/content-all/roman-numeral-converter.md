H1: Roman Numeral Converter
Roman numerals still appear on clock faces, book chapters, film copyright dates, monarchs' names, Super Bowl titles and tattoos. This converter turns ordinary (Arabic) numbers from 1 to 3,999 into Roman numerals, and Roman numerals back into numbers.
How to use this converter
1. Direction: "Arabic number → Roman" or "Roman → Arabic number".
2. Value: a whole number from 1 to 3,999, or a Roman numeral (capitals or lower case).
Result: the Roman numeral or the number.

The rules
1. Add symbols written from largest to smallest: VI = 5 + 1 = 6; LXX = 70.
2. Subtract a smaller symbol placed before a larger one, in six standard pairs only:

3. Don't repeat a symbol more than three times in a row (so 4 is IV, not IIII), and never repeat V, L or D.
How the conversion works
To go from a number to Roman, the converter repeatedly takes the largest value from the list M, CM, D, CD, C, XC, L, XL, X, IX, V, IV, I that still fits and subtracts it. To go back, it reads left to right, adding each value and treating the six subtractive pairs as single units.

Why stop at 3,999?
Standard Roman numerals have no symbol above M, and four Ms in a row breaks the three-repeat rule. Historically, a bar over a numeral (a vinculum) multiplied it by 1,000: V̄ = 5,000. There's also no Roman symbol for zero; medieval scholars wrote "nulla".
Where you'll see them
Monarchs and popes: Charles III, Elizabeth II, Leo XIV.
Super Bowl: Super Bowl LX (60) was played in February 2026.
Film and TV credits: copyright years such as MMXXVI.
Clocks: many clock faces use IIII instead of IV for visual balance, a tradition, not an error.
Outlines and book front matter: chapters, appendices and preface pages (i, ii, iii).
Tips and common mistakes
Invalid subtractions: 99 is XCIX, not IC; 49 is XLIX, not IL.
Too many repeats: 40 is XL, not XXXX.
Mixing up D and C, or L and C, when reading old inscriptions.
Tattoo dates: convert each part separately (day, month, year) and double-check before committing to ink.
Notes for US, Canada and UK users
Film and TV copyright dates in the US and UK traditionally use Roman numerals, such as MMXXVI for 2026.
Super Bowl numbers (US) and monarchs' names (UK: Charles III, Elizabeth II) are the most familiar modern uses.
Clocks: many clock faces in all three countries show IIII for 4 rather than IV, a long-standing design tradition. The converter uses the standard subtractive form, IV.
Outlines and prefaces: US and Canadian book prefaces often number pages i, ii, iii in lower-case Roman numerals; the values are the same.
