H1: CPP Contribution Calculator
Almost every worker in Canada outside Quebec contributes to the Canada Pension Plan (CPP) through payroll deductions or, if self-employed, on their tax return. This calculator works out your 2026 CPP contributions, including the second additional contribution (CPP2) on earnings above the first ceiling, and shows what your employer pays on top. It covers contributions only, not the pension you'll receive.
How to use this calculator
1. Annual employment or self-employment income: pensionable earnings for 2026, before deductions.
2. Employee or self-employed: self-employed people pay both the employee and employer shares.
3. Months covered (optional): if you turned 18, started receiving a CPP pension or became disabled during the year, the exemption and maximums are pro-rated.
Results: CPP contribution (base plus first additional), CPP2 contribution, total, employer contribution and, for the self-employed, the deductible portion.

The formula
CPP  = 5.95% × (min(Earnings, 74,600) − 3,500)
CPP2 = 4% × (min(Earnings, 85,000) − 74,600), if earnings exceed 74,600
Employers match both amounts. Self-employed people pay double.
Worked examples
Example 1: $60,000 salary
CPP = 5.95% × (60,000 − 3,500) = $3,361.75
CPP2 = $0 (earnings below the YMPE)
Of the CPP, the base portion (4.95%) is $2,796.75, which earns a non-refundable federal tax credit at 14% (about $391.55). The first additional portion (1%), $565.00, is a deduction from income.
Example 2: $80,000 salary
CPP = $4,230.45 (the maximum)
CPP2 = 4% × (80,000 − 74,600) = $216.00
Total employee contributions $4,446.45; the employer pays the same again.
Example 3: $100,000 salary
CPP $4,230.45 + CPP2 $416.00 = $4,646.45, the maximum employee total of $4,646.45.
Example 4: self-employed, $80,000 net business income
CPP = $8,460.90; CPP2 = $432.00; total $8,892.90
Half ($4,446.45) relates to the "employer" share, which is deductible from income; the rest is treated like employee contributions (credit and deduction as above).
Example 5: $30,000 part-time salary
CPP = 5.95% × (30,000 − 3,500) = $1,576.75
CPP2 = $0
Employer pays another $1,576.75
Example 6: turning 18 partway through the year
If you turn 18 in June, contributions apply only for the months after your birthday (July to December, 6 months). The basic exemption is pro-rated to 3,500 × 6 ÷ 12 = $1,750. On $20,000 earned in those months: 5.95% × (20,000 − 1,750) = $1,085.88 [VERIFY pro-rating method against CRA payroll guidance]. Enter 6 in "months covered" to see this.

Understanding your results
Contributions stop once you hit the maximums. Later paycheques in the year will have no CPP deducted.
Two jobs: each employer deducts CPP separately, so you may over-contribute. The excess employee contribution is refunded when you file your tax return; employer contributions aren't refunded.
Contributions are linked to your future pension. The enhancement (first and second additional contributions) is gradually raising the CPP retirement pension for people who contribute after 2019.
Take-home pay: CPP, EI and income tax all come off your pay; see the Canada take-home pay calculator.
Who contributes
Workers aged 18 to 70 with earnings above $3,500 outside Quebec.
You stop contributing at 70, or earlier if you're 65 to 70, receiving a CPP retirement pension and file an election (form CPT30 for employees) to stop.
Quebec workers contribute to the Quebec Pension Plan (QPP), administered by Retraite Québec with its own rates. This calculator doesn't cover the QPP.
What CPP pays
The CPP retirement pension can start from age 60 (reduced by 0.6% a month before 65) to 70 (increased by 0.7% a month after 65). The amount depends on how much and for how long you contributed. Service Canada's My Service Canada Account shows your contribution history and estimate. Compare the US system on the Social Security calculator.
Tips and common mistakes
Applying CPP to all income. Only earnings between $3,500 and the ceilings count; investment income doesn't.
Forgetting CPP2 on earnings above $74,600.
Self-employed underestimates. You pay both shares, which can be a large bill at tax time.
Not claiming an overpayment when you've had more than one employer.
Notes for US and UK readers
US: the equivalent is Social Security tax, 6.2% each for employee and employer on wages up to $184,500 in 2026, plus 1.45% Medicare with no cap.
UK: employees pay Class 1 National Insurance at 8% on earnings between £12,570 and £50,270 a year and 2% above, with employers paying 15% above a lower threshold [VERIFY 2026/27 rates].
Unlike CPP, neither has a basic exemption that comes off every worker's earnings in the same way, so comparisons at a given salary need care.
Frequently asked questions
What is the maximum CPP contribution for 2026?
$4,230.45 for employees, plus up to $416.00 in CPP2: $4,646.45 in total. Self-employed: $9,292.90.
What is CPP2?
The second additional CPP contribution: 4% on earnings between $74,600 and $85,000 in 2026.
Do I get CPP contributions back?
Only if you over-contributed (for example, with two employers). Otherwise they fund your future CPP benefits.
Are CPP contributions tax-deductible?
The enhanced portions are deducted from income; the base portion earns a non-refundable tax credit.
Do I pay CPP on self-employment income?
Yes, both shares, through your income tax return (Schedule 8).
Does Quebec use CPP?
No. Quebec has the QPP.
