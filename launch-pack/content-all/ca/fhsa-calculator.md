H1: FHSA Calculator
The First Home Savings Account (FHSA) lets first-time buyers in Canada save up to $40,000 for a home with a tax deduction on the way in and tax-free withdrawals on the way out, combining the best features of an RRSP and a TFSA. This calculator projects your FHSA balance, checks contributions against your participation room, and estimates the tax refunds your deductions could generate.
How to use this calculator
1. Year you opened your first FHSA. Room starts accumulating only once you open an account.
2. Planned contribution each year.
3. Unused room carried forward from the previous year (up to $8,000).
4. Expected annual return on your investments in the account.
5. Marginal tax rate: your combined federal and provincial rate, to estimate refunds. The Canada income tax calculator shows it.
6. Years until you buy.
Results: projected balance, total contributions, investment growth, estimated tax refunds and a warning if a contribution would exceed your room.
How the limits work
Annual participation room: $8,000 in the year you open your first FHSA.
Carry-forward: unused room from a year can be carried forward, but only up to $8,000. So the most you can put in during a single year is $16,000.
Lifetime limit: $40,000 in contributions (and transfers from RRSPs) across all your FHSAs.
Investment income doesn't use room. Growth inside the account isn't counted.
Over-contributions are taxed at 1% a month on the highest excess amount until removed.
Time limit: the account must be used or closed by the end of the 15th year after you first open one, or the year you turn 71, whichever is earlier [VERIFY wording on CRA]. Unused funds can be transferred to an RRSP or RRIF without affecting RRSP room.
Eligibility: Canadian resident, at least 18 (19 in some provinces) and under 72, and a first-time home buyer (you didn't live in a home you or your spouse or common-law partner owned at any time in the current year or the previous four calendar years).
The formula
Contributions at the start of each year, growing at rate r:
Balance after year k = (Balance after year k−1 + Contribution) × (1 + r)
Tax refund ≈ Contributions deducted × Marginal tax rate
You don't have to deduct contributions in the year you make them; you can carry deductions forward to a year when your income and tax rate are higher.
Worked examples
Example 1: maximum contributions for five years
$8,000 at the start of each year for five years, earning 5%:
Balance = $46,415.30
Contributions $40,000; growth $6,415.30
At a 30% marginal rate, deductions are worth about $12,000.00 in refunds.
After the refunds, the $46,415.30 you can withdraw tax-free cost you $28,000 out of pocket.
A couple who are both first-time buyers can each open an FHSA, doubling this to $92,830.61.
Example 2: opening the account and catching up
Open an FHSA in 2026 and contribute nothing that year. In 2027 you can contribute $16,000 ($8,000 new room plus $8,000 carried forward), then $8,000 a year for three years. At 5%, the balance after five years is $45,929.10. Opening the account early, even with a small amount, starts the room clock.
Using the money
A qualifying withdrawal to buy a first home is tax-free and doesn't need to be repaid. You need a written agreement to buy or build a qualifying home in Canada before 1 October of the year after the withdrawal, and you must intend to live in it within a year of buying. You can combine an FHSA withdrawal with the RRSP Home Buyers' Plan (up to $60,000 [VERIFY]), but HBP withdrawals must be repaid over 15 years (about $4,000.00 a year on $60,000) while FHSA withdrawals don't.
Non-qualifying withdrawals are added to your taxable income.

Tips and common mistakes
Waiting to open an account. Room only starts when you open one.
Carrying forward more than $8,000. Only $8,000 of unused room carries forward.
Contributing more than your room across multiple FHSAs.
Withdrawing before you have a written purchase agreement.
Holding cash for years. If your purchase is several years away, consider an investment mix that suits your timeline.
Checklist before you buy
Before withdrawing, confirm that you're a first-time home buyer, that you have a written agreement to buy or build a qualifying home in Canada before 1 October of the following year, and that you plan to live in it within a year. Fill in Form RC725 and give it to your FHSA issuer, so no tax is withheld from the withdrawal. If you also use the RRSP Home Buyers' Plan, keep a record of both withdrawals for your tax return.
Frequently asked questions
How much can I put in an FHSA?
$8,000 a year (up to $16,000 with carry-forward) and $40,000 in total.
Are FHSA contributions tax deductible?
Yes, and you can carry the deduction forward.
Is the FHSA withdrawal taxable?
Not if it's a qualifying withdrawal for a first home.
What happens if I don't buy a home?
You can transfer the balance to an RRSP or RRIF tax-free, without using RRSP room, or withdraw it as taxable income.
Can I have an FHSA and use the Home Buyers' Plan?
Yes, for the same home.
Does FHSA growth count toward the $40,000 limit?
No. Only contributions and RRSP transfers count.
