H1: Down Payment Calculator
This calculator converts a down payment percentage into a dollar amount and shows the mortgage you would need for the rest of the price. Enter the home price and the percentage you plan to put down. Use it to set a savings target, check whether you will need mortgage insurance, or compare 5%, 10% and 20% down on the same home.
How to use this calculator
1. Home price: the purchase price you expect to pay, or a list price you are considering.
2. Down payment percent: the share of the price you will pay from your own funds, e.g. 5, 10 or 20.
Results: down payment amount and loan amount.
The formula
Down payment = Price × Down% ÷ 100
Loan amount  = Price − Down payment
Loan-to-value (LTV) = Loan amount ÷ Price × 100 = 100 − Down%
LTV matters because lenders price loans and require mortgage insurance based on it.
Worked examples
Example 1: $400,000 home, 20% down
Down payment = 400,000 × 0.20 = $80,000.00
Loan amount = $320,000.00; LTV = 80%
At 20% down, a conventional US mortgage normally needs no private mortgage insurance.
Saving $80,000.00 at $1,500 a month in an account earning 4% a year (compounded monthly) would take about 50 months (4.1 years). The savings calculator runs this for your own figures.

Working backwards from the cash you have
If you have $60,000 and expect closing costs of about 3% of the price, and you want to put 10% down, the highest price you can cover is 60,000 ÷ (0.10 + 0.03) = $461,538.46 (down payment $46,153.85, closing costs $13,846.15). The calculator doesn't do this step yet, but the arithmetic is quick.
Understanding your results
Down payment amount is cash due at closing, on top of closing costs (which in the US often run a few percent of the price; see the closing cost calculator).
Loan amount is what you borrow before any financed fees. Use it in the mortgage calculator to get the monthly payment.
Bigger down payment: smaller loan, lower payment, less interest, and usually no mortgage insurance.
Smaller down payment: buy sooner and keep a cash cushion, but pay more interest and insurance.
Where the money can come from
Common sources are savings, proceeds from selling a current home, gifts from family, and some retirement or tax-advantaged accounts. Each comes with rules. Lenders want to see where large deposits came from, usually through two or more months of bank statements, so avoid moving money around just before you apply. Borrowed money (for example, a personal loan) generally can't be used as a down payment on a conventional mortgage, and it would raise your debt-to-income ratio anyway. Down payment assistance programs, run by some states, provinces and cities, can help first-time buyers with grants or deferred loans.
Country notes
United States
Conventional loans can allow as little as 3% down for some first-time buyers; FHA loans 3.5% with a qualifying credit score; VA and USDA loans can need nothing down for eligible borrowers [VERIFY current program minimums].
With less than 20% down on a conventional loan, lenders usually require private mortgage insurance (PMI). Under CFPB guidance, you can ask to cancel PMI once the balance reaches 80% of the original value, and it ends automatically at 78% (for most loans closed on or after 29 July 1999). Estimate it with the PMI calculator.
Canada
Minimum down payments are set by federal rules (FCAC):
up to $500,000: 5%;
$500,000 to under $1.5 million: 5% of the first $500,000 plus 10% of the rest;
$1.5 million or more: 20%.
For an $800,000 home, the minimum is C$55,000.00 (6.88% of the price). With less than 20% down, mortgage default insurance is required; see the CMHC insurance calculator. First-time buyers can use an FHSA or the RRSP Home Buyers' Plan towards the deposit.
United Kingdom
UK buyers call it a deposit. Mortgages are commonly offered from 5% or 10% deposit, with better rates at 25% and 40% (loan-to-value bands of 75% and 60%). The Lifetime ISA adds a 25% government bonus on up to £4,000 a year for first-time buyers, with a £450,000 property price cap [VERIFY cap]. Budget separately for Stamp Duty Land Tax in England and Northern Ireland (see the stamp duty calculator).
Tips and common mistakes
Forgetting closing costs. The down payment isn't the only cash you need.
Emptying your savings. Keep an emergency fund after closing; new homes bring unexpected bills.
Ignoring gift rules. Lenders usually need a gift letter for money from family.
Choosing 20% at any cost. Waiting years to save 20% can cost more than mortgage insurance if prices rise; compare both paths.
Frequently asked questions
Do I need 20% down to buy a house?
No. Many loans accept less, but in the US you'll usually pay mortgage insurance below 20% on a conventional loan, and in Canada insurance is required below 20%.
What is the minimum down payment in Canada?
5% on the first $500,000, 10% on the portion from $500,000 to $1.5 million, and 20% at $1.5 million or more.
Does the down payment include closing costs?
No. Closing costs are separate and are paid at closing in addition to the down payment.
What is loan-to-value?
The loan amount as a percentage of the property's value. 20% down equals 80% LTV.
Can I use gift money?
Usually yes, with documentation. Rules depend on the lender and loan program.
Is a bigger down payment always better?
It lowers your payment and interest, but tying up all your cash can leave you exposed. Balance the down payment with an emergency fund.
