H1: Mortgage Amortization Calculator
A mortgage amortization schedule shows how each monthly payment is split between interest and principal, and how your loan balance falls over the life of the mortgage. Enter the loan amount, rate and term to see the payment, the total interest and a chart of the balance, cumulative interest and cumulative payments. This page explains how to read the schedule and what it tells you about equity, refinancing and extra payments.
How to use this calculator
1. Loan amount: the mortgage principal, i.e. the price minus your down payment, plus any financed fees. It is on your Loan Estimate or mortgage statement.
2. Interest rate: the note rate (not the APR).
3. Loan term (years) and Loan term (months): usually 30 or 15 years.
Results: monthly payment (principal and interest), total of payments, total interest paid, and an amortization chart, with the first payments listed in a table.
The formula
PMT = L × r / (1 − (1 + r)^−n)
Interest(k)  = Balance(k−1) × r
Principal(k) = PMT − Interest(k)
Balance(k)   = Balance(k−1) − Principal(k)
L: loan amount; r: annual rate ÷ 12; n: months
Each month's interest is rounded to the cent, and the last payment is adjusted to clear the balance exactly.

Understanding your results
Front-loaded interest. Early payments are mostly interest because interest is charged on a large balance. That is not a penalty; it's how amortization works.
Equity build-up. Equity grows with each principal payment and with any rise in the home's value. Slow early principal is why selling within a few years often returns little equity once selling costs are paid.
Total interest assumes you keep the loan to the end. Most people move or refinance sooner.
Payment excludes escrow. Property tax, insurance and mortgage insurance are paid on top; they don't change the amortization.
Using the schedule
PMI removal (US): on a conventional loan, PMI ends automatically when the balance is scheduled to reach 78% of the original home value. If the $400,000 loan above was on a $500,000 home, that happens at month 26. You can ask to cancel at 80%. See the PMI calculator.
Refinancing: the schedule shows your balance at any point, which you need to compare a refinance; see the refinance calculator.
Extra payments: each extra dollar of principal skips future interest. The mortgage payoff calculator shows the time and interest saved.
Tax records (US): interest paid each year is reported on Form 1098 if you itemize.
Country notes
United States: most mortgages are fixed for 15 or 30 years, so a single schedule covers the whole loan.
Canada: mortgages are amortized over up to 25 years (30 for some insured first-time buyers and new builds [VERIFY current rules]), but the rate is usually fixed for a 5-year term and then renewed. Interest on fixed-rate mortgages is compounded semi-annually by law, so the monthly rate is slightly lower than rate ÷ 12. Use the Canadian mortgage calculator.
United Kingdom: "repayment" mortgages amortize like this, usually over 25 to 35 years, with fixed rates for 2 to 5 years before reverting. Interest-only mortgages don't amortize. See the UK mortgage calculator.
Tips and common mistakes
Using the APR as the rate. The schedule uses the note rate; APR includes fees.
Forgetting financed fees. If fees are added to the loan, include them in the amount.
Reading year 1 as typical. Interest falls and principal rises every year.
Comparing loans by payment only. A longer term cuts the payment but adds a lot of interest.
Quick check
In the first year of the $400,000 example, interest is well over three-quarters of each payment. Rerun it with a 15-year term to see how much faster equity builds.
Frequently asked questions
Why is most of my early mortgage payment interest?
Interest is charged on the outstanding balance, which is largest at the start. As the balance falls, more of each payment goes to principal.
How much of my loan will I have paid off after 5 years?
For the example above, the balance after 5 years is $374,444.08, so about $25,000 of principal has been repaid. Run your own loan to see your figure.
Does an extra payment change the schedule?
Yes. It reduces the balance immediately, so every later payment has more principal and less interest, and the loan ends sooner.
Is a 15-year mortgage better than a 30-year one?
It costs much less interest and usually has a lower rate, but a higher payment. The 30-year offers flexibility; you can still pay extra.
Why does my lender's schedule differ by a few cents?
Lenders round slightly differently and may use exact day counts for the first payment.
What is negative amortization?
When payments don't cover the interest, so the balance grows. Standard fixed-rate mortgages don't do this.
What is the difference between this and the general amortization calculator?
The maths is identical. This page focuses on mortgages: equity, PMI milestones, refinancing and country rules. For car loans and personal loans, use the amortization calculator.
