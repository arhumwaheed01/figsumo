H1: Scientific Notation Calculator
Scientific notation writes very large or very small numbers as a number between 1 and 10 multiplied by a power of ten. Avogadro's number, 602,000,000,000,000,000,000,000, becomes 6.02 × 10²³. This converter goes both ways: from an ordinary decimal to scientific notation, and from scientific notation back to a decimal.
How to use this converter
1. Mode: "Decimal → scientific notation" or "Scientific notation → decimal".
2. Value:
Decimal mode: type the number (for example 0.000123).
Scientific mode: type it as 6.02e23, 6.02x10^23, 6.02×10^23 or 6.02*10^23. Negative exponents work: 1.5e-3.
Result: the converted number.
How it works
N = a × 10ⁿ,   where 1 ≤ |a| < 10 and n is an integer
n = floor(log₁₀ |N|)
a = N ÷ 10ⁿ
In words: count how many places the decimal point moves to leave one non-zero digit in front of it. Moving left gives a positive exponent; moving right gives a negative one.
Worked examples
Example 1: a large number
602,000,000,000,000,000,000,000 → the point moves 23 places left → 6.02 × 10²³
Example 2: a small number
0.000123 → the point moves 4 places right → 1.23 × 10⁻⁴
Example 3: back to decimal
1.5e-3 → move the point 3 places left → 0.0015
Example 4: multiplying
To multiply, multiply the coefficients and add the exponents: (6.02 × 10²³) × (3.0 × 10⁻²) = 18.06 × 10²¹ = 1.806 × 10²². (Check: 18,059,999,999,999,998,951,424.)
Example 5: dividing
Divide the coefficients and subtract the exponents: (8 × 10⁶) ÷ (2 × 10²) = 4 × 10⁴.

Notation variants
E-notation (calculators, spreadsheets, code): 6.02E23. The "E" means "× 10 to the power", not the constant e.
Engineering notation: exponents in multiples of 3, matching SI prefixes: 12,500 = 12.5 × 10³ (12.5 k).
Normalised form: the coefficient has exactly one non-zero digit before the point. 60.2 × 10²² is correct in value but not normalised.
Understanding your results
Significant figures: the coefficient shows the precision. 6.02 × 10²³ has three sf; 6.020 × 10²³ has four.
Coefficients are shown to six decimals at most; use the rounding calculator for a specific number of sf.
Precision limits: standard numbers carry about 15–17 significant digits, so extremely long decimals are rounded. The big number calculator handles exact large integers.
Tips and common mistakes
Moving the point the wrong way for small numbers (the exponent should be negative).
Forgetting to re-normalise after multiplying (18.06 × 10²¹ → 1.806 × 10²²).
Adding with different exponents. Convert to the same power first: 3 × 10⁴ + 2 × 10³ = 3.2 × 10⁴.
Typing "e" for the constant when you mean a power of ten.
Notes for US, Canada and UK readers
US and Canadian schools usually say "scientific notation"; UK schools say "standard form" (GCSE) or "standard index form". They mean the same thing: a number from 1 to just under 10, multiplied by a power of ten. "Engineering notation", where the exponent is a multiple of 3, is used in all three countries in technical work.
Practice problems
1. Write 0.000 045 in scientific notation: 4.5 × 10⁻⁵.
2. (3 × 10⁴) × (5 × 10³) = 15 × 10⁷ = 1.5 × 10⁸.
3. (8.4 × 10⁶) ÷ (2.1 × 10²) = 4 × 10⁴.
4. Two moles of a substance contain 2 × 6.022 × 10²³ = 1.2044 × 10²⁴ particles.
Frequently asked questions
How do I write 0.00056 in scientific notation?
5.6 × 10⁻⁴.
What does 3.2e5 mean?
3.2 × 10⁵ = 320,000.
Can the coefficient be 10?
No. It must be at least 1 and less than 10.
How do I multiply numbers in scientific notation?
Multiply the coefficients, add the exponents, then normalise.
What is engineering notation?
Scientific notation with exponents that are multiples of 3.
Which input formats are accepted?
e-notation, "x10^", "×10^" and "*10^".
