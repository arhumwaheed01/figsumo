H1: Rent Affordability Calculator
How much rent can you afford? This calculator applies a percentage-of-income rule to your monthly income and returns a rent budget. The common starting point is 30% of gross income, but the right share depends on your other costs, debts and savings goals, so the page also explains the tests landlords use and how to check the result against a full budget.
How to use this calculator
1. Monthly income: your gross (before-tax) monthly income. Include every regular source you can document: salary, self-employment, benefits. For a household, combine incomes if everyone will be on the lease.
2. Percent of income for rent: the share you want to spend. The default is 30%.
Result: suggested monthly rent.
The formula
Rent budget = Monthly income × Percent ÷ 100
Worked examples
Example 1: the 30% rule
Gross monthly income $6,000.00 (about $72,000.00 a year)
Rent budget = 6,000 × 0.30 = $1,800.00
Example 2: comparing percentages
For $4,500 gross a month:
25%: $1,125.00
30%: $1,350.00
35%: $1,575.00
Example 3: the 50/30/20 check on take-home pay
If the same household's take-home pay is $4,580 a month, the 50/30/20 approach allows $2,290.00 for all needs (rent, utilities, groceries, transport, minimum debt payments), $1,374.00 for wants and $916.00 for savings and extra debt repayment. Rent has to fit inside the needs share alongside everything else.
Example 4: a UK renter on £45,000
30% of gross monthly income: 3,750 × 0.30 = £1,125.00
Letting-agent rule of 30 times the monthly rent: 45,000 ÷ 30 = £1,500.00
The agent's test allows a higher rent than the 30% guide, but passing referencing doesn't make a rent comfortable. Use the lower figure as your budget.
Example 5: flatmates in Canada
Two people earning $3,900 and $3,900 a month combined ($7,800) and both on the lease: 30% is $2,340.00. If one earns more, split the rent in proportion to income or equally, but agree it in writing.
Example 6: utilities paid separately
If the 30% budget is $1,800 and electricity, internet and renter's insurance come to about $200 a month, the most you should offer the landlord is $1,600.00.

Understanding your results
The result is a ceiling, not a target. Living below it frees money for savings and emergencies.
Add utilities and renter's insurance. If they're not included in rent, they reduce what you can pay the landlord.
High-cost cities. In many large cities, renters spend well over 30%. The US Department of Housing and Urban Development calls households paying more than 30% of income on housing "cost-burdened" and over 50% "severely cost-burdened".
Use net income for a stricter test. 30% of take-home pay is noticeably less than 30% of gross. The take-home pay calculator gives your net figure.
What landlords look for
Income multiple: many US landlords want annual gross income of about 40 times the monthly rent. On $72,000.00 a year that's a maximum rent of $1,800.00; a $2,100 apartment needs $84,000.00 a year.
UK: letting agents commonly require annual income of around 30 times the monthly rent (£63,000.00 for £2,100 a month), and may ask for a guarantor otherwise.
Credit and references: credit history, rental history and employment checks are standard. Debt payments matter too; see the debt-to-income calculator.
Country notes
United States: HUD's 30% benchmark is used for affordability programs. Security deposit limits and rent control vary by state and city.
Canada: CMHC treats housing costing less than 30% of before-tax household income as affordable. On $5,200 a month, 32% would be $1,664.00. Several provinces (including Ontario and British Columbia) set annual limits on rent increases for existing tenancies [VERIFY 2026 guideline amounts].
United Kingdom: in England, the Tenant Fees Act caps tenancy deposits at five weeks' rent for annual rent below £50,000, and deposits must be protected in a government-approved scheme. Rents are typically quoted per calendar month (pcm).
Plan for rent increases
A lease renewal at 3% a year takes a $1,800 rent to $1,854.00 after one year and $2,086.69 after five. If your income doesn't keep pace, the share of income going on rent creeps up. In Ontario and British Columbia, rent increases for most existing tenancies are capped by a provincial guideline each year; in most US states and in England there's no general cap, though notice rules apply [VERIFY current local rules].
Tips and common mistakes
Forgetting move-in costs: deposit, first month, moving and furniture.
Using income you can't document. Landlords usually need proof.
Ignoring commuting costs. A cheaper flat further out may cost more overall.
Not leaving room for savings. An emergency fund protects you from falling behind on rent.
Frequently asked questions
What is the 30% rule for rent?
Spend no more than 30% of gross monthly income on rent. It's a guideline from US housing policy, not a law.
Should I use gross or net income?
Landlords use gross. For your own budget, check the rent against take-home pay too.
How much income do I need for a $2,000 apartment?
At 30% of gross, about $6,667 a month ($80,000 a year). Under the 40× rule, $80,000 a year.
Does rent include utilities?
Sometimes. Check the listing and add any utilities you'll pay separately.
What if rent is more than 30% of my income?
Many people pay more, especially in big cities. Cut other costs, consider roommates, or look at the budget calculator to see what fits.
Is renting or buying cheaper?
It depends on prices, rates and how long you'll stay. Try the rent vs buy calculator.
