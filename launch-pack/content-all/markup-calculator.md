H1: Markup Calculator
Markup is how much you add to the cost of a product to set its selling price, expressed as a percentage of cost. Retailers, wholesalers, contractors and makers use it to price consistently. This calculator works out the selling price from cost and markup, the markup from cost and price, or the cost from price and markup, and shows the gross profit and gross margin for each.
How to use this calculator
Enter any two of the three values:
1. Cost: what the item costs you (purchase price, or materials and labour).
2. Markup (%): the percentage you add to cost.
3. Selling price: the price before sales tax or VAT.
Results: the missing value, plus gross profit (price − cost) and gross margin (profit as a percentage of price).
The formulas
Selling price = Cost × (1 + Markup)
Markup = (Price − Cost) ÷ Cost
Cost = Price ÷ (1 + Markup)
Gross profit = Price − Cost
Margin = (Price − Cost) ÷ Price
Converting between the two:
Margin = Markup ÷ (1 + Markup)
Markup = Margin ÷ (1 − Margin)
Worked examples
Example 1: pricing from cost
Cost $40; markup 50%.
Price = 40 × 1.5 = $60.00
Profit $20.00; margin 20 ÷ 60 = 33.33%
Example 2: finding the markup
Cost $40; you sell at $55.
Markup = 15 ÷ 40 = 37.50%
Margin = 15 ÷ 55 = 27.27%
Example 3: working back to the maximum cost
You want to sell at $80 with a 60% markup.
Maximum cost = 80 ÷ 1.6 = $50.00
Example 4: markup on a margin target
A 40% margin needs a markup of 0.4 ÷ 0.6 = 66.67%. A 25% markup gives a margin of only 2000.00%.

A 100% markup (doubling the cost, often called "keystone" pricing in retail) is a 50% margin. A $3.20 item at a 150% markup sells for $8.00.
Chained markups
A wholesaler buys at $18.50 and adds 20% ($22.20); a retailer adds 25% to that. The shelf price is $27.75. Markups multiply rather than add: 20% then 25% is 50% overall, not 45%.
Understanding your results
Markup is on cost; margin is on price. The same profit gives a bigger markup percentage than margin percentage. Mixing them up is the most common pricing mistake. See the margin calculator.
Gross profit isn't net profit. Rent, wages, marketing and other overheads still need covering; the break-even calculator shows how many units you need to sell.
Price before tax. Add US sales tax at checkout, or include VAT/GST in UK and Canadian consumer prices; see the VAT calculator.
Discounts cut margin faster than you might expect: a 20% discount on a 50% markup leaves a 20% markup (16.67% margin).
Choosing a markup
Markups vary hugely by industry: groceries operate on thin margins, while jewellery and restaurant drinks carry high markups. Consider:
What competitors charge
Your overheads per unit
Volume: high-volume items can carry lower markups
Perceived value and brand
Payment processing and returns costs
Tips and common mistakes
Calculating margin as markup (or the reverse).
Leaving costs out: shipping in, packaging, card fees.
Adding markups instead of multiplying them when there are several stages.
Ignoring tax in consumer-facing prices where it must be included.
Frequently asked questions
What is the formula for markup?
Markup = (Price − Cost) ÷ Cost × 100.
What's the difference between markup and margin?
Markup is profit as a percentage of cost; margin is profit as a percentage of the selling price.
What markup gives a 50% margin?
100%.
How do I add a 30% markup?
Multiply the cost by 1.3.
Is a 50% markup the same as a 50% margin?
No. A 50% markup is a 33.33% margin.
Should markup include overheads?
Your markup needs to be high enough that total gross profit covers overheads and leaves a profit.
