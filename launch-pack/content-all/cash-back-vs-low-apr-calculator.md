H1: Cash Back vs Low APR Calculator
Car makers often offer a choice: take a cash rebate off the price, or skip the rebate and get a low promotional interest rate. This calculator compares the total you would pay under each option over the same loan term and tells you which is cheaper and by how much.
How to use this calculator
1. Vehicle price: the negotiated price you will finance, before the rebate.
2. Cash rebate: the cash-back amount offered instead of the special rate. The calculator subtracts it from the amount financed.
3. Standard loan APR (no rebate): the rate you would get if you take the rebate, for example from your bank, credit union or the dealer's standard financing. Get a pre-approval so this figure is real.
4. Promotional APR (with rebate forfeited): the special rate offered if you give up the rebate, e.g. 0% or 1.9%.
5. Loan term (years): the same term is used for both options so the comparison is fair. Check that the promotional rate is available for the term you enter; low-rate deals are often limited to shorter terms.
Results: total cost of each option, the better option (lower total) and the savings.
The formula
Both options use the standard loan payment:
PMT = P × r / (1 − (1 + r)^−n)
Option A (rebate):  P = Price − Rebate, at the standard APR
Option B (low APR): P = Price, at the promotional APR
Total cost = PMT × n
r: APR ÷ 12; n: years × 12
The option with the lower total cost wins; savings = the difference between the two totals.
The comparison assumes no down payment, no trade-in and no sales tax, and that you keep the loan to the end.
Worked examples
Example 1: $35,000 car, $2,000 rebate or 1.9%, standard rate 6.9%, 5 years
Rebate: finance $33,000 at 6.9% → $651.88 a month, total $39,113.02
Low APR: finance $35,000 at 1.9% → $611.94 a month, total $36,716.50
Take the low APR; it saves $2,396.52
Example 2: $28,000 car, $3,500 rebate or 2.9%, standard rate 5.5%, 5 years
Rebate: finance $24,500 at 5.5% → $467.98 a month, total $28,078.71
Low APR: finance $28,000 at 2.9% → $501.88 a month, total $30,112.80
Better option: the cash rebate, saving $2,034.09
Break-even: the promotional rate would have to be below about 0.11% to beat the rebate here
The bigger the rebate and the lower the standard rate you can get, the more likely the rebate wins.
Understanding your results
Total cost is the sum of all monthly payments. It does not include any down payment, tax or fees, which are the same under both options in most deals.
Savings is the difference in total payments. The monthly payment also differs, so check the cheaper option fits your budget.
Paying cash? If you aren't financing at all, the rebate is pure savings and always wins.
Things the simple comparison misses
Sales tax on the rebate. In many US states, sales tax is charged on the price before a manufacturer rebate, so the rebate doesn't reduce the tax bill [VERIFY your state's rule]. Since both options then pay the same tax, the comparison above still holds.
Paying off early. If you expect to sell or pay off the car early, the low rate's advantage shrinks, because the interest saving builds up over the term while the rebate is immediate.
Shorter promotional terms. If the low APR only applies to 36 months, compare on 36 months. At 36 months, Example 1 costs $36,627.70 with the rebate and $36,034.67 with the 1.9% rate.
Credit approval. Promotional rates usually need top-tier credit. If you don't qualify, the rebate may be your only real option.
Negotiation. Agree the price first; ask about incentives afterwards.
Getting a real standard rate
The comparison is only as good as the standard APR you enter. Before visiting the dealer, apply for a pre-approval with a bank or credit union; it gives you a rate based on your actual credit file, usually valid for a few weeks. With that in hand, you can enter a real figure here, and you can ask the dealer to beat it. If you skip this step and guess a rate that is too high, the low-APR option will look better than it really is.
Country notes
United States: the Truth in Lending Act requires the APR and finance charge to be disclosed before you sign. The FTC warns buyers to watch for add-ons bundled into dealer financing.
Canada: manufacturer incentives work the same way; GST/HST and PST apply to the purchase price. Provincial consumer protection offices regulate dealer disclosure.
United Kingdom: "0% APR" or "cashback" offers are common with PCP and hire-purchase agreements. Compare the total amount payable shown on the finance quote.
Tips and common mistakes
Use a real standard rate. Get a loan quote from a bank or credit union before visiting the dealer.
Match the term. Comparing a 72-month rebate loan with a 36-month promotional loan is not like-for-like.
Don't count the rebate twice. It is already subtracted from the amount financed.
Check for extras. Some promotional deals require buying add-ons or using dealer financing with fees.
Frequently asked questions
Is 0% financing always better than a rebate?
No. It depends on the rebate size, your standard rate and the term. Large rebates combined with a decent standard rate often beat 0%.
What if I put money down?
Subtract the down payment from the vehicle price before entering it. The comparison remains valid as long as the down payment is the same under both options.
Does the calculator include sales tax?
No. Tax is usually the same under both options, so it does not change which one is cheaper.
Why is my monthly payment lower with the low APR even though I borrow more?
A large rate difference can outweigh the extra principal. Compare totals as well as payments.
Can I take both the rebate and the low rate?
Sometimes manufacturers allow it on certain models. If so, take both.
What is the break-even rate?
The promotional APR at which both options cost the same. Below it, the low APR wins.
