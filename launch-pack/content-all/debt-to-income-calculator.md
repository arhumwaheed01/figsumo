H1: Debt-to-Income Ratio Calculator
Your debt-to-income ratio (DTI) is the share of your gross monthly income that goes to debt payments. Lenders use it to judge whether you can take on a new loan, especially a mortgage. This calculator gives two versions: the front-end ratio (housing costs only) and the back-end ratio (housing plus all other debts).
How to use this calculator
1. Monthly gross income: income before tax and deductions. For a salary, divide the annual figure by 12. Include regular, documentable income such as bonuses, self-employment profit or benefits if a lender would count them. With a co-borrower, add both incomes.
2. Monthly housing payment: rent, or the mortgage payment including property tax, homeowners insurance, mortgage insurance and HOA dues if you're testing a mortgage application. Your Loan Estimate shows the full monthly figure.
3. Other monthly debt payments: minimum payments on credit cards, car loans, student loans, personal loans, child support and other recurring debts that appear on a credit report or court order. Don't include utilities, groceries, insurance premiums or phone bills.
Results: front-end DTI (housing) and back-end DTI (all debts), as percentages.
The formula
Front-end DTI = Housing payment ÷ Gross monthly income × 100
Back-end DTI  = (Housing payment + Other debt payments) ÷ Gross monthly income × 100
The CFPB defines DTI as all monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income, which is the back-end ratio here.
Worked examples
Example 1: income $8,000, housing $1,800, other debts $600
Front-end = 1,800 ÷ 8,000 = 22.5%
Back-end = (1,800 + 600) ÷ 8,000 = 30.0%
Example 2: $72,000 salary, planned mortgage $1,650, car $420, student loan $250, card minimum $95
Gross monthly income = 72,000 ÷ 12 = $6,000.00
Other debts = $765.00
Front-end = 27.5%; back-end = 40.2%
At a 36% back-end ceiling (a commonly used rule of thumb, not a legal limit), total debts could be up to $2,160.00, leaving −$255.00 of headroom
Paying off the car loan before applying would bring the back-end ratio down to 33.2%
Understanding your results
Lower is better. A lower DTI means more of your income is free after debt payments, which lenders read as lower risk.
There is no single cut-off. As the CFPB notes, different loan products and lenders have different DTI limits. Rules of thumb you'll see include 28% front-end and 36% back-end for conventional loans; government-backed loans and automated underwriting can accept higher ratios when other factors are strong [VERIFY current FHA/Fannie Mae limits before citing figures].
DTI is only one factor. Credit history, down payment, savings (reserves) and employment stability also matter.
It ignores living costs. A DTI that a lender accepts can still be tight for your budget. Check the result in the budget calculator.
How to lower your DTI
Pay down or pay off a debt with a high monthly payment, such as a car loan.
Avoid new credit before applying for a mortgage.
Look at a cheaper home or larger down payment, which lowers the housing payment.
Add a co-borrower's income, if they will share responsibility for the loan.
Refinance or consolidate to lower monthly payments, keeping in mind that a longer term can cost more interest.
What lenders count as income and debt
Lenders count income they can verify and expect to continue: salary on pay stubs and W-2s or T4s, two-year averages of bonuses, overtime and self-employment profit (after business expenses), pensions, and some benefits. One-off payments and cash income without records usually don't count. On the debt side, they take the payments from your credit report plus the proposed housing payment. A card you pay off in full each month can still count at its minimum payment if a balance is reported. Before applying, pull your credit reports so you know exactly what a lender will see.
Country notes
United States: DTI is central to mortgage underwriting. Qualified Mortgage rules, FHA, VA and the guidelines of Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac each treat DTI differently. VA loans also use a "residual income" test.
Canada: lenders use the Gross Debt Service (GDS) ratio (housing costs including property tax, heating and half of condo fees, divided by gross income) and the Total Debt Service (TDS) ratio (GDS plus other debts). For insured mortgages the commonly cited limits are 39% GDS and 44% TDS [VERIFY with CMHC]. Payments are tested at the higher of the contract rate plus 2% or 5.25% under the OSFI stress test. See the Canadian mortgage affordability calculator.
United Kingdom: lenders focus on affordability assessments under FCA rules and loan-to-income limits (the Bank of England limits how many mortgages lenders can make at 4.5 times income or more). DTI-style ratios appear in lender calculations but are rarely quoted to borrowers.
Tips and common mistakes
Using net instead of gross income. DTI uses gross (pre-tax) income.
Leaving out the full housing cost. Include property tax, insurance and HOA dues, not just principal and interest.
Counting the full card balance. Use the minimum monthly payment shown on the statement.
Forgetting co-signed loans. Lenders usually count them as your debt.
Frequently asked questions
What is a good debt-to-income ratio?
Lower is better. Many lenders are comfortable with a back-end ratio in the 30s or below, but limits vary by lender and loan type.
Is DTI based on gross or net income?
Gross income, before taxes and deductions.
Do utilities and insurance count as debt?
No. Only debt payments and housing costs count. Homeowners insurance is included as part of the housing payment for a mortgage.
What is the difference between front-end and back-end DTI?
Front-end includes only housing costs. Back-end includes housing plus all other monthly debt payments.
Does DTI affect my credit score?
No. Credit scores don't use income. But lenders look at DTI alongside your score.
How do I calculate DTI with irregular income?
Lenders usually average two years of documented income for self-employment or variable pay. Use a cautious monthly average.
