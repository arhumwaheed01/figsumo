H1: Self-Employment Tax Calculator
If you work for yourself in the United States, you pay Social Security and Medicare tax through self-employment (SE) tax, covering both the employee and employer shares that would normally be split with an employer. This calculator estimates your 2026 SE tax from your net profit, including the Social Security wage base cap and the Additional Medicare Tax, and shows the half you can deduct.
How to use this calculator
1. Net self-employment profit: income minus business expenses, from Schedule C (or your share of partnership earnings from Schedule K-1).
2. W-2 wages (optional): if you also have a job, wages subject to Social Security tax reduce the room left under the wage base.
3. Filing status: for the Additional Medicare Tax threshold.
Results: net earnings from self-employment, Social Security portion, Medicare portion, total SE tax, deductible half and Additional Medicare Tax (if any).
The formula (2026)
Net earnings (NE) = Net profit × 92.35%
Social Security part = 12.4% × min(NE, 184,500 − W-2 wages)
Medicare part = 2.9% × NE
SE tax = Social Security part + Medicare part
Deduction = SE tax ÷ 2
92.35% (100% − 7.65%) mirrors the fact that employees don't pay FICA on the employer's share.
$184,500 is the 2026 Social Security wage base.
Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% applies to combined wages and SE income above $200,000 (single, head of household), $250,000 (married filing jointly) or $125,000 (married filing separately). It's reported on Form 8959 and isn't part of the deductible half.
No SE tax is due if net earnings are under $400.
Worked examples
Example 1: $80,000 net profit
NE = 80,000 × 0.9235 = $73,880.00
SE tax = 15.3% × $73,880.00 = $11,303.64 (14.13% of profit)
Deductible half: $5,651.82, which reduces your adjusted gross income for income tax
An employee earning $80,000 would see only $6,120.00 withheld for FICA; the employer pays the other half, and SE tax combines both.
Example 2: $250,000 net profit, single
NE = $230,875.00
Social Security: 12.4% × 184,500 = $22,878.00 (capped)
Medicare: 2.9% × $230,875.00 = $6,695.38
SE tax = $29,573.38
Additional Medicare Tax: 0.9% × (NE − 200,000) = $277.88
Example 3: a job plus a side business
W-2 wages $150,000 and SE profit $60,000:
NE = $55,410.00; room under the wage base = 184,500 − 150,000 = $34,500
Social Security: 12.4% × 34,500 = $4,278.00
Medicare: 2.9% × $55,410.00 = $1,606.89
SE tax = $5,884.89
Example 4: a $30,000 side business, no other job
NE = 30,000 × 0.9235 = $27,705.00
SE tax = 15.3% × $27,705.00 = $4,238.86
Deductible half: $2,119.43
Example 5: below the $400 threshold
Net profit of $420 gives net earnings of 420 × 0.9235 = $387.87, which is under $400, so no SE tax is due. You may still need to report the income for income tax.

Paying SE tax
Estimated taxes: you generally need to make quarterly estimated payments (Form 1040-ES) covering both income tax and SE tax if you expect to owe $1,000 or more. If your SE tax is $11,304 and your income tax is $8,000, quarterly payments of about $4,825.91 would cover the year.
Due dates: usually April 15, June 15, September 15 and January 15.
Safe harbor: paying 100% of last year's tax (110% if your AGI was over $150,000) in timely installments generally avoids an underpayment penalty.
Understanding your results
SE tax is in addition to income tax. Your total tax is SE tax plus federal (and state) income tax on profit after deductions. See the income tax calculator.
Retirement contributions (SEP IRA, solo 401(k)) reduce income tax but not SE tax.
The qualified business income deduction can reduce income tax on pass-through profit; it doesn't reduce SE tax.
S corporations: owners who take a salary pay FICA through payroll and receive remaining profit as distributions not subject to SE tax, but the salary must be reasonable.
Tips and common mistakes
Applying 15.3% to the whole profit. Use 92.35% of profit first.
Forgetting the wage base when you also have W-2 wages.
Skipping estimated payments, which can lead to penalties.
Not tracking expenses. Every legitimate deductible expense lowers both income tax and SE tax.
Notes for Canada and UK readers
Self-employed people in other countries face a similar "both halves" rule:
Canada: self-employed workers pay both the employee and employer CPP contributions, 11.9% on earnings between $3,500 and $74,600 in 2026, plus 8% CPP2 above that up to $85,000. See the CPP calculator.
UK: self-employed people pay Class 4 National Insurance on profits, at a main rate of 6% between £12,570 and £50,270 and 2% above [VERIFY 2026/27 rates]. Class 2 contributions are no longer compulsory but can be paid voluntarily to protect State Pension entitlement.
US citizens living abroad may still owe SE tax unless a totalization agreement assigns their coverage to the other country; the US has such agreements with both Canada and the UK.
Frequently asked questions
What is the self-employment tax rate for 2026?
15.3%: 12.4% Social Security (up to the $184,500 wage base) and 2.9% Medicare.
Do I pay SE tax on all my profit?
On 92.35% of it, with the Social Security part capped at the wage base.
Is SE tax deductible?
Half of it is deductible when figuring adjusted gross income.
When do I have to pay SE tax?
If net earnings are $400 or more. You'll typically pay through quarterly estimated taxes.
Does SE tax count toward Social Security benefits?
Yes. It earns credits and counts toward your benefit, like FICA on wages.
Do side gigs count?
Yes. Freelance and gig income is self-employment income.
