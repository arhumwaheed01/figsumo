H1: W-4 Withholding Estimator
Form W-4 tells your employer how much federal income tax to withhold from each paycheck. If too little is withheld, you'll owe at tax time and possibly an underpayment penalty; too much, and you're giving the government an interest-free loan until your refund arrives. This calculator projects your 2026 federal income tax, compares it with what you're on track to have withheld, and suggests an extra amount per paycheck for Step 4(c) of your W-4.
How to use this calculator
1. Filing status: single, married filing jointly or head of household.
2. Pay frequency and annual wages (from all jobs; for two-earner couples, include both).
3. Pre-tax deductions: 401(k), HSA and Section 125 health premiums reduce taxable wages.
4. Other income: interest, dividends, side income not subject to withholding.
5. Deductions: itemized deductions above the standard deduction, if any.
6. Credits: for example the Child Tax Credit ($2,200 per qualifying child in 2026).
7. Federal tax withheld so far this year, paychecks remaining and withholding per paycheck (from your latest pay stub).
Results: projected 2026 tax, projected total withholding, amount you'd owe or get back, and the extra withholding per paycheck to break even.
How it works
Taxable income = Wages − Pre-tax deductions + Other income − Standard (or itemized) deduction
Projected tax = Tax from 2026 brackets − Credits
Projected withholding = Withheld to date + Per-paycheck withholding × Paychecks left
Extra per paycheck = (Projected tax − Projected withholding) ÷ Paychecks left
2026 standard deduction: $16,100 single, $32,200 married filing jointly, $24,150 head of household.
2026 single brackets: 10% to $12,400; 12% to $50,400; 22% to $105,700; 24% to $201,775; 32% to $256,225; 35% to $640,600; 37% above.
Worked examples
Example 1: single, $75,000 salary, mid-year check
Taxable income = 75,000 − 16,100 = $58,900
Projected tax = $7,670.00 (10.23% of wages)
After 18 of 26 bi-weekly paychecks, $4,800 has been withheld; the stub shows $230 a paycheck, so 8 more add $1,840.
Projected withholding = $6,640.00, a shortfall of $1,030.00
Extra withholding = $1,030.00 ÷ 8 = $128.75 per paycheck in Step 4(c)
Example 2: married filing jointly, $120,000, two children
Taxable income = 120,000 − 32,200 = $87,800.00
Tax before credits $10,040.00; Child Tax Credit 2 × $2,200 = $4,400
Tax after credits = $5,640.00
If withholding for the year is on track to be $5,640.00 plus $3,600, the couple would get about a $3,600.00 refund. They could reduce withholding by entering the credits in Step 3 of the W-4 to keep more in each paycheck instead.
Example 3: two jobs, both with default W-4s
A single filer earns $60,000 at one job and $30,000 at another:
Tax on the combined $90,000: taxable income $73,900, tax $10,970.00
Job 1 withholds as if $60,000 were the only income: about $5,020.00
Job 2 also applies the full standard deduction and the low brackets: about $1,420.00
Total withheld ≈ $6,440.00, a shortfall of about $4,530.00
Spread over 26 paychecks, that's $174.23 of extra withholding per paycheck, entered on one job's W-4 in Step 4(c), or use the Step 2 checkbox on both W-4s.
Example 4: a bonus withheld at 22%
A $5,000 bonus withheld at the 22% supplemental rate has $1,100.00 of federal tax taken. If your top bracket is 12%, the actual tax on the bonus is about $600.00, so the extra $500.00 comes back as part of your refund.
The W-4 steps
Step 1: personal information and filing status.
Step 2: multiple jobs or a working spouse. Use the IRS estimator, the worksheet, or tick the box if there are only two jobs with similar pay.
Step 3: dependants and other credits.
Step 4(a): other income without withholding. 4(b): deductions beyond the standard deduction. 4(c): extra withholding per paycheck.
Step 5: sign.
Since 2020 the W-4 doesn't use allowances. You can submit a new W-4 at any time; changes usually apply from the next payroll.
Understanding your results
A projection, not a return. It's based on the year's figures you enter; a bonus, job change or new child can change it.
Underpayment penalty safe harbors: you generally avoid a penalty if withholding and timely estimated payments cover at least 90% of this year's tax or 100% of last year's (110% if last year's AGI was over $150,000).
Bonuses are often withheld at a flat 22% supplemental rate, which may be too little or too much for you.
State withholding is separate and uses state forms.
Tips and common mistakes
Two jobs, two default W-4s. Each employer withholds as if it's your only job, often leaving you short. Use Step 2.
Forgetting side income that has no withholding.
Not updating after life changes: marriage, divorce, a new child or buying a home.
Claiming credits twice when both spouses enter them on their W-4s.
Notes for Canadian and UK workers
Canada: the equivalent form is the TD1 (federal plus a provincial version), which sets your personal tax credits. If you have two jobs, you generally claim the basic personal amount with only one employer, and you can request extra deductions on the form.
UK: there's no form to fill in each year. HMRC issues a tax code to each employer, such as 1257L for the standard Personal Allowance, and second jobs usually get a BR code (all taxed at the basic rate). Check your code in your HMRC personal tax account if your pay looks wrong.
Frequently asked questions
How do I know if I'm withholding enough?
Compare projected withholding with projected tax using your latest pay stub, as this calculator does.
What do I put in Step 4(c)?
The extra amount per paycheck needed to cover any shortfall.
Can I change my W-4 mid-year?
Yes, at any time, by giving your employer a new form.
Is it better to get a refund or owe?
Owing a small amount means you kept your money during the year, but owing too much can bring penalties.
Does the W-4 affect Social Security and Medicare?
No. FICA is withheld at fixed rates regardless of the W-4.
Where can I get an official estimate?
The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator on irs.gov.
