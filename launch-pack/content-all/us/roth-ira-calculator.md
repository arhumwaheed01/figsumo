H1: Roth IRA Calculator (2026)
A Roth IRA is funded with money you've already paid tax on. In return, qualified withdrawals, including all the growth, are tax-free. This calculator does two jobs. It checks how much you're allowed to contribute for 2026, because the limit shrinks at higher incomes. Then it projects how the account could grow and how much of the final balance would be tax-free growth.
How to use this calculator
1. Filing status and modified adjusted gross income (MAGI). These determine whether your limit is reduced.
2. Age: at 50 or older, a catch-up amount is added.
3. Taxable compensation: you can't contribute more than you earned from work. A spouse with little or no income may be able to use the other spouse's compensation when filing jointly.
4. Current Roth balance, yearly contribution, expected return and years for the growth projection.
5. Optional comparison: enter a tax rate to compare with investing the same amount in a taxable account.
The formula
2026 contribution limit: $7,500, plus $1,100 if you're 50 or older ($8,600). The limit is shared across all your traditional and Roth IRAs.

Inside the range, the IRS worksheet in Publication 590-A reduces the limit in proportion:
Reduced limit = Limit − Limit × (MAGI − lower threshold) ÷ (range width)
The result is rounded up to the next $10. If it is above $0 but below $200, you can contribute $200.
Growth (annual contributions at year-end):
FV = B × (1 + r)^n + C × ((1 + r)^n − 1) / r
B: current balance
C: yearly contribution
r: annual return
n: years
Worked examples
Example 1: maximum contributions for 25 years. $7,500 a year, 7% average annual return, starting from $0.
FV = 7,500 × (1.07^25 − 1) / 0.07 = $474,367.78
Contributions: 25 × 7,500 = $187,500
Tax-free growth: $286,867.78, provided withdrawals are qualified
The contribution limit has been raised over time for inflation. Keeping it flat at $7,500 makes this estimate conservative on that point.
Example 2: partial contribution in the phase-out. Single filer under 50, MAGI $160,000.
Into the range by: 160,000 − 153,000 = $7,000 of a $15,000 range
Reduction: 7,500 × 7,000 ÷ 15,000 = $3,500
Maximum Roth contribution: $4,000 (already a multiple of $10)
At MAGI $168,000 or more, the direct Roth contribution limit is $0.
Understanding your results
Allowed contribution: your 2026 limit after the age, compensation and income tests.
Projected balance: split into contributions (which you can generally withdraw at any time without tax or penalty) and earnings (tax-free only for qualified distributions).
Comparison with a taxable account: shows the effect of paying no tax on growth, using your tax rate. It's a simplification; real taxable accounts depend on the mix of dividends, capital gains and holding periods.
Converting a traditional IRA to a Roth
A conversion moves money from a traditional IRA into a Roth IRA. The pre-tax amount you convert is added to your taxable income for that year. Converting $10,000 at a 22% marginal rate adds about $2,200 of federal tax. There are no income limits on conversions. Each conversion has its own five-year period for the 10% additional tax if the converted amount is withdrawn before age 59½. The calculator models contributions only; Publication 590-B explains conversions in full.
Country notes
US only. Similar tax-free accounts: Canada's TFSA and the UK's ISA, with different limits and rules.
Timing: contributions for 2026 can be made until the tax-filing deadline in 2027 (not including extensions).
Tips and common mistakes
Contributing above the income limit. Excess contributions face a 6% excise tax each year until fixed.
Confusing the five-year rules. There's one for earnings and another for each conversion.
Missing the deadline. Contributions for a tax year can be made until the filing deadline the following April.
Leaving contributions in cash. Money must be invested inside the account to grow.
Assuming Roth is always better. If your tax rate in retirement will be lower, a traditional account may win.
Frequently asked questions
What is the Roth IRA limit for 2026?
$7,500, or $8,600 if you're 50 or older by the end of the year. It's a combined limit for all your traditional and Roth IRAs, and it can't exceed your taxable compensation.
What are the 2026 income limits?
For single and head-of-household filers, the phase-out range is $153,000–$168,000 of MAGI. For married filing jointly, it is $242,000–$252,000.
When are Roth withdrawals tax-free?
Earnings are tax-free in a qualified distribution, which generally means the account has been open for at least five tax years and you are 59½ or older (or meet another qualifying condition, such as disability). Your own contributions can be withdrawn at any time without tax or penalty.
Can I contribute to a Roth IRA and a 401(k)?
Yes. The IRA limit is separate from the 401(k) deferral limit. Your income still has to be within the Roth IRA limits.
What if I contribute too much?
Excess contributions are subject to a 6% excise tax for each year they stay in the account. You can avoid it by withdrawing the excess, and any earnings on it, by your return's due date (including extensions).
Is a Roth better than a traditional IRA?
A Roth tends to suit you better if you expect a similar or higher tax rate in retirement. A traditional IRA tends to suit you better if you expect a lower rate. Deductibility of traditional IRA contributions also depends on income and workplace plan coverage.
