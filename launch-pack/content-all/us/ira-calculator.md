H1: IRA Calculator
This calculator projects the balance of a traditional IRA at retirement and estimates what it's worth after income tax on withdrawal. Enter your current balance, how much you'll add each year, the years until you withdraw, an expected return and the tax rate you expect in retirement. For 2026, the IRA contribution limit is $7,500, plus a $1,100 catch-up if you're 50 or older (IRS, IR-2025-111).
How to use this calculator
1. Current balance: the value of your traditional IRA today, from your latest statement.
2. Annual contribution: what you'll put in each year. The 2026 limit is $7,500 ($8,600 at 50+), and you can't contribute more than your taxable compensation for the year. The limit is shared across all your traditional and Roth IRAs.
3. Years until withdrawal: how long until you start taking money out.
4. Expected return: the yearly growth you assume after fund fees.
5. Tax rate at withdrawal: your expected average tax rate on IRA withdrawals in retirement (federal plus state).
Results: pre-tax balance and after-tax balance (estimate).
The formula
Pre-tax balance = B × (1 + g)^n + C × ((1 + g)^n − 1) ÷ g
After-tax balance = Pre-tax balance × (1 − Tax rate)
B: current balance; C: annual contribution; g: expected return; n: years
Growth compounds annually, and contributions are added at the end of each year.
Traditional IRA withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income (except any after-tax basis from nondeductible contributions), so applying your expected tax rate to the whole balance gives a rough spendable value.
Worked examples
Example 1: $20,000 balance, $7,500 a year for 20 years at 7%, 22% tax in retirement
Pre-tax balance = $384,859.88
After 22% tax: $300,190.71 (tax of $84,669.17)
Your own money in: $170,000.00
If the contributions are deductible and you're in the 22% bracket today, each $7,500 contribution also cuts this year's tax by about $1,650.00.
Example 2: starting at 50 with the catch-up
$0 today, $8,600 a year for 15 years at 6.5%, 12% expected tax rate:
Pre-tax balance: $207,966.66
After tax: $183,010.66
The value of starting early
Two savers each contribute $7,500 a year at 7%. One starts 30 years before retirement, the other 20 years before. The early starter ends with $708,455.90 pre-tax; the later starter with $307,466.19. The extra 10 years of contributions ($75,000) produce $400,989.71 more, because the early money has longer to compound.
Understanding your results
Pre-tax balance is the account value before any withdrawals.
After-tax balance is a simple estimate. In practice you withdraw over many years, and each year's withdrawals are taxed at that year's rates.
Returns vary. Use a cautious return and test a range.
Required minimum distributions (RMDs) start at age 73 for most people (75 for those born in 1960 or later [VERIFY]); see the RMD calculator.
Traditional or Roth?
Traditional IRA: contributions may be tax-deductible now; withdrawals are taxed later. Better if you expect a lower tax rate in retirement.
Roth IRA: no deduction now; qualified withdrawals are tax-free. Better if you expect a higher or similar rate later. Income limits apply to direct Roth contributions (2026 phase-out: $153,000–$168,000 single, $242,000–$252,000 married filing jointly). Compare with the Roth IRA calculator.
If your tax rate is the same now and later, a deductible traditional IRA and a Roth IRA give the same after-tax result for the same pre-tax contribution.
Deduction rules
You can always contribute to a traditional IRA if you have earned income, but the deduction phases out at certain incomes if you (or your spouse) are covered by a workplace retirement plan. The 2026 phase-out ranges are published in IRS Notice 2025-67 [VERIFY ranges before quoting]. If you can't deduct, contributions are "nondeductible" and you must track the basis on Form 8606.
Early withdrawals
Withdrawals before age 59½ are generally subject to a 10% additional tax on top of income tax, unless an exception applies (for example, first-time home purchase up to $10,000, qualified higher-education costs, or certain medical expenses). See IRS Publication 590-B.
Country notes
The IRA is US-only. In Canada, the closest equivalent is the RRSP (deductible now, taxed on withdrawal); see the RRSP calculator. In the UK, a personal pension (SIPP) works similarly, with tax relief on contributions and 25% usually tax-free at withdrawal; see the workplace pension calculator.
Tips and common mistakes
Contributing over the limit. Excess contributions face a 6% excise tax each year until corrected.
Missing the deadline. You can contribute for 2026 until the tax filing deadline in April 2027.
Ignoring fees. Lower the expected return by fund costs.
Forgetting state tax in the withdrawal tax rate.
Frequently asked questions
What is the IRA contribution limit for 2026?
$7,500, or $8,600 if you're 50 or older by the end of the year, across all your traditional and Roth IRAs combined.
Are traditional IRA contributions tax-deductible?
Often, yes. If you or your spouse have a workplace plan, the deduction phases out above certain incomes.
When do I have to start withdrawing?
Required minimum distributions generally begin at 73 under current law.
How is the IRA taxed when I withdraw?
Withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income, except for any nondeductible contributions (basis).
Does the calculator compound monthly?
No. It compounds annually and adds contributions at the end of each year.
Can I have an IRA and a 401(k)?
Yes. Workplace plan coverage can limit the traditional IRA deduction but not the right to contribute.
Can I contribute to an IRA if I don't work?
You need taxable compensation, but a spouse filing jointly can make a spousal IRA contribution based on the working spouse's compensation.
