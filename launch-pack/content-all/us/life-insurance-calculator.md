H1: Life Insurance Needs Calculator
How much life insurance do you need? The answer depends on what your family would have to pay for without your income: daily living costs for a number of years, the mortgage and other debts, children's education and final expenses, minus what you already have. This calculator uses that needs-based approach to estimate a coverage amount. It doesn't quote premiums or recommend a product.
How to use this calculator
1. Income to replace (per year): often less than your full salary, because your own living costs and taxes would stop. Many people use 70% to 80% of take-home or gross pay.
2. Years of support: until your youngest child is independent, or your partner reaches retirement.
3. Real rate of return (optional): if you want the present value of the income stream (the lump sum that, invested, would pay it out).
4. Mortgage balance and other debts: car loans, student loans, credit cards.
5. Final expenses: funeral and estate costs.
6. Education fund: future college or university costs you want covered.
7. Existing life insurance (including group cover through work) and savings and investments available to your family.
Results: total needs, resources available and additional coverage needed.
The formula
Needs = Income replacement + Mortgage + Debts + Final expenses + Education
Coverage needed = Needs − Existing life insurance − Savings and investments
Income replacement can be calculated two ways:
Simple: annual amount × years.
Present value: annual amount × (1 − (1 + r)^−n) ÷ r × (1 + r), with payments at the start of each year and r a real (after-inflation) return. This is the lump sum that would fund the payments.
Worked example
Earner on $70,000 a year, partner and two children; replace 80% ($56,000.00 a year) for 15 years.
Income replacement, simple method: $840,000.00
Present value at a 2% real return: $733,949.93
Mortgage: $240,000; other debts: $15,000; final expenses: $12,000; education fund: $80,000
Total needs: $1,080,949.93
Existing group life cover $100,000 and savings $60,000: $160,000.00
Additional coverage needed: $920,949.93
A common shortcut, 10 times salary, would suggest $700,000.00. The needs-based figure is more tailored because it accounts for the mortgage, education goals and existing assets.
Example 2: a stay-at-home parent
No salary, but replacing childcare and household help would cost about $15,000 a year until the youngest child is 12, in 10 years:
Present value at 2% real: 15,000 × (1 − 1.02⁻¹⁰) ÷ 0.02 × 1.02 = $137,433.55
Final expenses $12,000
Coverage needed ≈ $149,433.55, less any existing cover
Example 3: a single earner with a mortgage and no dependants
If nobody relies on your income, needs may be just the debts others would inherit responsibility for (often none, if loans are only in your name) and final expenses. Many single people without dependants need little or no individual cover, beyond any free group cover through work.

Understanding your results
Term vs permanent: for income replacement over a fixed period, many families look at term life insurance, which covers a set number of years. Permanent (whole or universal) life insurance lasts for life and builds cash value at a higher cost. Which suits you depends on your goals.
Stay-at-home parents: include the cost of childcare and household work, even without a salary.
Social Security survivor benefits may replace part of the income for children under 18 and a surviving spouse caring for them; you can treat an estimate as a resource. See the Social Security calculator.
Group cover through work often ends when you leave the job; don't rely on it entirely.
Review the figure after major life events: marriage, a new child, a home purchase or a pay rise.
Tax notes
Life insurance death benefits are generally not subject to federal income tax for the beneficiary. Proceeds can be included in the insured's estate for estate tax purposes if the insured owned the policy, which matters only for large estates (the 2026 basic exclusion is $15 million); see the estate tax calculator.
Canada and the UK
The same needs-based method works elsewhere. In Canada, life insurance proceeds are generally tax-free to beneficiaries, and the CPP pays a death benefit and survivor's pension. In the UK, policies written in trust usually keep proceeds outside the estate for Inheritance Tax; employer "death in service" cover is common.
Tips and common mistakes
Using gross salary for 30 years without considering that needs fall as children grow.
Forgetting debts or counting the mortgage twice (once in income and once as a lump sum).
Ignoring inflation in long time frames; use a real return.
Insuring only the main earner.
Overlooking existing assets, which reduce the need.
Frequently asked questions
How much life insurance do I need?
Enough to cover your family's needs (income replacement, debts, education and final costs) minus the resources they'd already have.
Is 10 times my salary enough?
It's a rough rule of thumb. A needs-based calculation is more accurate for your situation.
Should I include my mortgage?
Yes, if you want your family to be able to pay it off.
Are life insurance payouts taxable?
Generally not for federal income tax, though interest on delayed payouts is taxable.
Do I need life insurance if I'm single without dependants?
Possibly not, beyond final expenses or debts someone else would have to pay.
How often should I review my coverage?
After major life changes and every few years.
