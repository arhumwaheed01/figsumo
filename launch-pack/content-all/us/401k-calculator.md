H1: 401(k) Calculator (2026 Limits)
A 401(k) combines three sources of growth: your own salary deferrals, your employer's match, and investment returns that aren't taxed until withdrawal (traditional) or ever (qualified Roth). This calculator projects the balance at retirement under the 2026 IRS limits. It shows how much comes from the match, and it warns you if your contribution rate is too low to get the full match.
How to use this calculator
1. Current age, retirement age and current 401(k) balance.
2. Annual salary and expected yearly raise.
3. Your contribution as a percent of salary. The calculator caps it at the IRS limit for your age.
4. Employer match formula, for example "100% of the first 4%" or "50% of the first 6%". You can add a second tier.
5. Expected annual return and contribution frequency (per paycheck or annual).
6. Vesting (optional): the percent of employer money you'd keep if you left today.
The formula
Each year y:
Salary_y      = Salary_0 × (1 + raise)^y
Employee_y    = min(contribution % × Salary_y, deferral limit for age)
Match_y       = match rate × min(contribution %, match cap %) × Salary_y
Total_y       = min(Employee_y + Match_y, §415(c) limit) (+ catch-up, which sits outside §415(c))
Balance_(y+1) = Balance_y × (1 + return) + Total_y        (annual mode, contributions at year-end)
In per-paycheck mode, contributions are added each pay period and compounded at the equivalent periodic rate. This gives a slightly higher result than annual mode.
2026 limits (IRS):
Employee elective deferral: $24,500
Catch-up, age 50+: $8,000, for a total of $32,500
Higher catch-up at ages 60–63: $11,250 instead of $8,000, for a total of $35,750
Total annual additions (employee + employer, excluding catch-up): $72,000
Worked examples
Example 1: getting the full match. Age 40, $10,000 balance, $70,000 salary (no raises, to keep the arithmetic clear), 6% contribution, employer matches 100% of the first 4%, 7% annual return, 25 years, annual mode.
Your contribution: 6% × 70,000 = $4,200 a year
Match: 100% × 4% × 70,000 = $2,800 a year
Balance at 65: 10,000 × 1.07^25 + 7,000 × (1.07^25 − 1) / 0.07 = $497,017.59
Without the match, the same plan would reach $319,920.28. The match adds $177,097.31.
Example 2: maximising, and age-based catch-up.
At a $150,000 salary, reaching the 2026 deferral limit of $24,500 takes 24,500 ÷ 150,000 = 16.33% of pay.
At age 61, the limit is 24,500 + 11,250 = $35,750.
Employer contributions count toward the $72,000 total-additions limit. Catch-up contributions are not included in that limit.
Understanding your results
Balance at retirement: split into your contributions, employer contributions and investment growth.
Match left on the table: if your rate is below the match cap, the calculator shows the yearly match you're missing.
Limit warning: shown when your percentage would exceed the deferral limit. Some plans stop deferrals at the limit, which can also stop the match for the rest of the year unless the plan has a "true-up". Ask your plan administrator.
Real value: add inflation to see the balance in today's dollars.
Taxes not deducted: traditional 401(k) withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income. Qualified Roth 401(k) withdrawals are tax-free.
The cost of early withdrawal
Taking $20,000 out at age 45 from a traditional 401(k), with a 22% federal marginal rate:
Federal income tax: 22% × 20,000 = $4,400
10% additional tax: $2,000
You keep about $13,600 before any state tax, and lose the future growth on the $20,000.
Exceptions to the 10% additional tax exist, for example separation from service in or after the year you turn 55. Check IRS Topic 558.
Required minimum distributions
You generally must start taking RMDs from traditional 401(k)s and IRAs when you reach age 73. Workplace-plan participants can usually delay until the year they retire, unless they are a 5% owner of the business. Roth 401(k) accounts are no longer subject to lifetime RMDs.
Country notes
US only. Similar accounts elsewhere: Canada's RRSP and group RRSPs; UK workplace pensions under auto-enrolment.
Roth catch-up rule: under SECURE 2.0, if your FICA wages from this employer were over $150,000 in 2025, any catch-up contributions you make in 2026 must be Roth (after-tax) contributions.
Tips and common mistakes
Leaving the match on the table. Contribute at least enough to get the full employer match; it's an immediate return no investment can promise.
Forgetting the vesting schedule. Employer contributions may not be fully yours until you've worked a set number of years.
Over-contributing with two employers. The $24,500 employee limit is per person, not per plan, so add up deferrals at every job.
Ignoring fund fees. An expense ratio difference of 0.5% a year compounds into a large sum over decades.
Cashing out when you change jobs. Roll the balance into the new plan or an IRA to avoid tax and the 10% early-withdrawal penalty.
Frequently asked questions
What is the 401(k) contribution limit for 2026?
$24,500 for employee deferrals. People aged 50 or older can add $8,000, and people aged 60–63 can add $11,250 instead. Total employee plus employer additions are capped at $72,000, not counting catch-up.
Is the employer match included in the $24,500?
No. The $24,500 limit applies only to your own deferrals. Employer contributions count toward the separate $72,000 total limit.
Should I choose traditional or Roth 401(k)?
Traditional lowers your taxable income now. Roth contributions are taxed now, but qualified withdrawals are tax-free. The better choice depends on your current and expected future tax rates. Many people split between the two. This calculator shows the balance for either; tax outcomes are personal.
What happens if I withdraw early?
Distributions before age 59½ are generally subject to income tax plus a 10% additional tax, unless an exception applies. Plan loans and hardship withdrawals follow plan rules.
What does vesting mean?
Your own contributions are always 100% yours. Employer contributions may vest over several years. If you leave before you're fully vested, you forfeit the unvested part.
Why does contribution frequency change the result?
Money invested each paycheck starts growing sooner than money added once a year. Over decades, that adds a little to the balance.
