H1: Social Security Calculator
Your Social Security retirement benefit depends on your lifetime earnings and the age you start claiming. This calculator estimates the monthly benefit from your average indexed monthly earnings (AIME), using the Social Security Administration's benefit formula, and adjusts it for claiming before or after your full retirement age (FRA).
How to use this calculator
1. Average indexed monthly earnings (AIME): your highest 35 years of earnings, adjusted for wage growth, divided by 420 months. Your SSA statement at ssa.gov/myaccount shows your earnings record; the SSA's own calculators compute AIME for you.
2. Claim age: the age you plan to start benefits, from 62 to 70.
3. Full retirement age: 67 if you were born in 1960 or later; between 66 and 67 for earlier birth years.
Results: estimated monthly benefit, primary insurance amount (PIA) at full retirement age, and a note.
The formula
Step 1: primary insurance amount. For people turning 62 in 2026, the PIA is:
PIA = 90% of AIME up to $1,286
    + 32% of AIME from $1,286 to $7,749
    + 15% of AIME above $7,749
The two thresholds are the bend points, set for the year you turn 62. They rise each year with average wages. The formula replaces a larger share of income for lower earners.
Step 2: adjust for claiming age.
Early: benefits fall by 5/9 of 1% for each of the first 36 months before FRA, and 5/12 of 1% for each month beyond 36. Claiming at 62 with an FRA of 67 cuts the benefit by 30%.
Delayed: delayed retirement credits add 2/3 of 1% for each month after FRA (8% a year), up to age 70.
Worked examples
Example 1: AIME $5,500, claiming at 67
90% × 1,286 = $1,157.40
32% × (5,500 − 1,286) = $1,348.48
PIA = $2,505.88 a month at full retirement age, about 46% of AIME.
Claiming at other ages:
At 62: $1,754.12 (30% less)
At 65: $2,171.76
At 70: $3,107.29 (24% more)
Example 2: AIME $9,000
$1,157.40 + $2,068.16 + 15% × (9,000 − 7,749) = $187.65
PIA = $3,413.21, about 38% of AIME. Higher earners get a smaller replacement rate.
When does waiting pay off?
Comparing claiming at 62 and 70 for Example 1 and ignoring cost-of-living adjustments and investment returns, the larger checks from 70 make up for the eight years of missed payments at about age 80.4. If you expect to live past that, delaying pays more over your lifetime; health, other income and a spouse's benefits matter too.

Example 3: a spousal benefit
A spouse with little work history can receive up to 50% of the worker's full-retirement-age benefit, claimed at the spouse's own full retirement age. With the worker's $2,000, that's up to $1,000 a month. Claiming the spousal benefit early reduces it, but unlike the worker's own benefit, it doesn't grow after full retirement age.
Understanding your results
Today's dollars, roughly. The estimate doesn't include future cost-of-living adjustments (COLAs), which raise benefits each year after you become eligible.
The PIA is rounded down to the next lower dime by SSA; the tool shows the unrounded figure.
Not everyone qualifies. You need 40 credits (about 10 years of covered work) for a retirement benefit.
Spousal and survivor benefits aren't included. A spouse can receive up to 50% of your PIA at their full retirement age.
Taxes and work
Social Security tax: employees pay 6.2% on wages up to the wage base ($184,500 in 2026), matched by the employer; the self-employed pay both halves.
Earnings test: if you claim before FRA and keep working, benefits are temporarily withheld when earnings exceed an annual limit [VERIFY 2026 limits]. Withheld benefits are credited back at FRA.
Income tax on benefits: up to 85% of benefits can be taxable depending on your combined income. The base thresholds ($25,000 single, $32,000 married filing jointly) aren't indexed.
Country notes
This calculator covers the US program. Canada pays the CPP (see the CPP calculator) and Old Age Security; the UK pays the State Pension, based on National Insurance years rather than earnings. The US has totalization agreements with both countries so periods of work can count toward eligibility.
Tips and common mistakes
Using current salary as AIME. AIME averages 35 years, including any zero years.
Assuming FRA is 65. It's 67 for anyone born in 1960 or later.
Forgetting the spouse. For married couples, the higher earner delaying increases the survivor benefit.
Claiming early while working without checking the earnings test.
Frequently asked questions
How is my Social Security benefit calculated?
SSA indexes your earnings, averages the top 35 years into an AIME, applies the bend-point formula to get the PIA, and adjusts for your claiming age.
What is my full retirement age?
67 if born in 1960 or later.
How much less do I get at 62?
30% less than your PIA if your FRA is 67.
How much more do I get by waiting until 70?
8% a year after FRA, so 24% more at 70 with an FRA of 67.
Do benefits go up with inflation?
Yes, through annual COLAs.
Where can I get my official estimate?
From your my Social Security account at ssa.gov.
