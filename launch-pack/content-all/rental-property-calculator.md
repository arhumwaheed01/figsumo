H1: Rental Property Calculator
Before buying a rental, you need to know whether the rent will cover the mortgage and running costs, and by how much. This calculator takes the monthly rent and the main monthly costs and returns the monthly cash flow, the annual net operating income (NOI) and the operating expense ratio.
How to use this calculator
1. Monthly rent: the rent you expect to collect. Check comparable listings rather than relying on the seller's figure.
2. Mortgage payment: monthly principal and interest. Use the mortgage calculator to estimate it.
3. Taxes and insurance (monthly): property tax plus landlord insurance, divided by 12.
4. Other monthly costs: maintenance, management, HOA or service charges, utilities you pay, and a vacancy allowance.
Results: monthly cash flow, net operating income (annual) and expense ratio.
The formulas
Cash flow (monthly) = Rent − (Mortgage + Taxes and insurance + Other costs)
NOI (annual) = (Rent − Taxes and insurance − Other costs) × 12
Expense ratio = (Taxes and insurance + Other costs) ÷ Rent
NOI excludes the mortgage, so it measures the property's performance regardless of financing. Cash flow includes the mortgage, so it's what lands in your account each month.
Worked examples
Example 1: the default figures
Rent $2,200; mortgage $1,400; taxes and insurance $350; other costs $200.
Cash flow = 2,200 − 1,950 = $250.00 a month ($3,000.00 a year)
NOI = (2,200 − 350 − 200) × 12 = $19,800.00
Expense ratio = 550 ÷ 2,200 = 25%
If the property cost $300,000 and you put in $70,000 cash (down payment and closing costs):
Cap rate = $19,800.00 ÷ 300,000 = 6.60%
Cash-on-cash return = $3,000.00 ÷ 70,000 = 4.29%
Example 2: adding vacancy and maintenance
Allow 5% vacancy (rent effectively $2,090.00) and 8% of rent for maintenance on top of the $200 (other costs $376.00):
Cash flow = −$36.00 a month (−$432.00 a year)
NOI = $16,368.00
A deal that looked positive becomes negative once realistic allowances are included. Enter the vacancy and maintenance allowances in "Other monthly costs" to see this in the tool.
Example 3: a UK higher-rate landlord
Rent £1,200 a month, mortgage interest £500 a month (interest-only), other costs £200 a month:
Pre-tax cash flow = (1,200 − 500 − 200) × 12 = £6,000 a year
Taxable rental profit (interest not deductible) = 14,400 − 2,400 = £12,000
Tax at 40% = £4,800, less a 20% credit on £6,000 of interest (£1,200) = £3,600
After-tax cash flow = £2,400
If interest were fully deductible, as it was before 2017, the tax would be £2,400. The restriction turns a 40%-taxpayer's £6,000 profit into £2,400 after tax, which is why many UK landlords now buy through limited companies [VERIFY whether that suits your situation with an adviser].
Example 4: quick screening rules
The 1% rule: some US investors look for monthly rent of at least 1% of the price. Example 1's $2,200 on a $300,000 property is 0.73%, below that guide.
Debt service coverage ratio (DSCR): NOI ÷ annual mortgage payments = 19,800 ÷ 16,800 = 1.18. Lenders that size loans on rental income often look for at least about 1.2 [VERIFY lender requirements].
Rules of thumb only screen deals; the full cash flow calculation decides.
Understanding your results
Positive cash flow means the rent covers all costs including the mortgage. Negative means you'll top up each month, betting on appreciation or loan paydown.
NOI is what lenders and buyers use to value a property; see the cap rate calculator.
Expense ratio: operating costs as a share of rent (mortgage excluded). Many investors budget roughly 35% to 50% for long-term residential rentals once vacancy, repairs, capital reserves and management are counted [VERIFY ranges for your market]. A very low ratio usually means something has been left out.
Cash flow isn't total return. Mortgage principal repayment and any price growth add to wealth; tax, transaction costs and major repairs subtract.
Costs people forget
Vacancy between tenants and letting fees
Repairs and capital items (roof, boiler/furnace, appliances)
Property management, often 8% to 12% of rent
Licensing, safety certificates and inspections
Legal costs and eviction risk
Country notes
United States: rental income and expenses go on Schedule E. Residential rental buildings are depreciated over 27.5 years, which reduces taxable income (but not cash flow). Some cities require rental registration.
Canada: report rental income on form T776. Capital cost allowance (CCA) is optional and can be recaptured on sale. Some cities, including Vancouver and Toronto, charge vacant-home taxes.
United Kingdom: individual landlords can't deduct mortgage interest from rental profits; they get a tax credit at 20% of finance costs instead. Buy-to-let lenders test rent against the mortgage at a stressed rate; see the buy-to-let mortgage calculator. Deposits must be protected and landlords need gas safety certificates and EPCs.
Tips and common mistakes
Assuming 100% occupancy. Budget for vacancy.
Using an interest-only payment when the loan is repayment, or the other way round.
Leaving out insurance increases and property tax reassessment after purchase.
Counting tax depreciation as cash. It's an accounting deduction.
Frequently asked questions
What is a good cash flow for a rental?
There's no universal number; many investors want a positive cash flow after realistic vacancy and repairs, with a cushion for surprises.
What's the difference between NOI and cash flow?
NOI is before mortgage payments; cash flow is after.
What is the 1% rule?
A rough screen that monthly rent should be at least 1% of the purchase price. It ignores taxes, insurance and rates, so use it only as a first filter.
What is cash-on-cash return?
Annual cash flow divided by the cash you invested.
Should I include my own labor?
Yes. If you self-manage, price your time as a management fee to compare properties fairly.
How is rental income taxed?
Net rental profit is taxable income in the US, Canada and the UK, with rules that differ by country.
