H1: Refinance Calculator
Refinancing replaces your current loan with a new one, usually to get a lower rate, change the term or take out cash. A lower payment isn't the whole story. Closing costs take time to earn back, and a longer new term can raise the total you pay even at a lower rate. This calculator puts the current loan and the new offer side by side, so you can see the monthly saving, the break-even month and the lifetime cost.
How to use this calculator
1. Current loan: remaining balance (from your latest statement), interest rate and remaining term.
2. New loan: offered rate, new term, and the closing costs from the lender's Loan Estimate.
3. Points (optional): discount points you pay to buy down the rate. One point is 1% of the new loan amount.
4. Cash-out (optional): extra cash you take, added to the new loan.
5. Pay costs upfront or roll them in: rolling costs into the loan increases the new balance.
The formula
Both payments use the standard formula:
PMT = B × r / (1 − (1 + r)^−n)
B: balance (current) or new loan amount (balance + cash-out + any financed costs)
r: annual rate ÷ 12
n: remaining months (current) or new term in months
Then:
Monthly saving       = Current PMT − New PMT
Break-even (months)  = Upfront costs ÷ Monthly saving
Lifetime difference  = (Current PMT × remaining months) − (New PMT × new months + upfront costs)
The break-even figure assumes you stay in the home and keep the new loan at least that long. If the monthly saving is zero or negative, there is no break-even point.
Worked examples
Example 1: lower rate, reset to 30 years. Current: $300,000 balance at 7%, 27 years (324 months) left. New: $300,000 at 6% for 30 years, $6,000 closing costs paid upfront.
Current payment: $2,063.44
New payment: $1,798.65
Monthly saving: $264.79
Break-even: 6,000 ÷ 264.79 = 22.66 months, so in the 23rd month
Remaining interest: current loan $368,556.11; new loan $347,514.57
The new loan saves $21,041.54 in interest, or $15,041.54 after the $6,000 of costs. You also make payments for three more years.
Example 2: lower rate, shorter term. The same refinance on a 25-year term:
New payment: $1,932.90, still $130.54 less than today
Total interest: $279,871.26, which is $88,684.85 less than keeping the current loan
Paid off two years earlier than the current loan
Here the trade-off is a smaller monthly saving (so a slower break-even, about 46 months on the same $6,000) for a much bigger lifetime saving.
Understanding your results
Monthly saving: the change in principal and interest. Escrow for tax and insurance is usually unchanged.
Break-even month: when the cumulative savings first exceed the upfront costs. If you might sell or refinance again before then, the refinance probably costs more than it saves.
Lifetime cost comparison: includes the closing costs, so you can see whether the lower rate really wins over the whole period.
Cash-out: a larger new balance raises the payment and the interest. The calculator shows that cost separately so you can judge the cash on its own terms.
Refinancing other kinds of debt
The same break-even logic works for any loan:
Auto loans: a lower rate can cut the payment, but stretching the term may increase total interest.
Student loans: in the US, refinancing federal loans with a private lender ends federal benefits such as income-driven repayment options. Weigh that before comparing rates.
Credit cards: a personal loan or balance transfer at a lower rate can reduce interest; include transfer fees as upfront costs.
Enter the remaining balance, rate and months of the existing debt as the "current loan" to compare.
Country notes
United States: your Loan Estimate lists the closing costs; compare offers using the same page. Points can be worth it if you'll keep the loan past the point break-even.
Canada: breaking a mortgage term early can cost a prepayment penalty, often three months' interest or an interest rate differential. Ask your lender, and enter the penalty as an upfront cost.
UK: remortgaging during a fixed deal may trigger an early repayment charge. Add it, plus any arrangement and legal fees, as upfront costs.
Tips and common mistakes
Looking only at the monthly saving. Check the break-even point against how long you'll stay.
Restarting the clock. A new 30-year loan on a mortgage with 22 years left can cost more interest overall.
Ignoring closing costs. Rolling them into the loan raises the balance and interest.
Forgetting prepayment penalties on the existing loan.
Refinancing federal student loans casually. You give up federal protections.
Frequently asked questions
How much lower does my rate need to be to refinance?
There's no fixed rule. What matters is how quickly the monthly saving pays back the costs, compared with how long you expect to keep the loan. The calculator's break-even figure answers that for your numbers.
Why could a lower rate cost me more overall?
If you restart a long term, you pay interest for more months. Always compare lifetime cost, not just the monthly payment.
Should I roll closing costs into the loan?
It avoids paying cash now, but you pay interest on those costs for the life of the loan. The calculator shows both versions.
What are discount points?
They are an upfront fee, 1% of the loan per point, paid for a lower rate. Divide the cost of the points by the monthly saving they create to get their own break-even point.
Does refinancing affect my credit?
Applying usually involves a credit check. The new loan replaces the old one on your credit report. Rate-shopping within a short window is treated differently by scoring models; ask the lender.
What about cash-out refinancing?
It increases your loan balance and the interest you pay, and it reduces your home equity. Compare its cost with other ways of borrowing before you choose it.
