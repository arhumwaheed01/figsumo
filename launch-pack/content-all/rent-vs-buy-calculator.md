H1: Rent vs Buy Calculator
Is it cheaper to rent or to buy where you live? The answer depends on home prices, mortgage rates, how fast rents rise and, above all, how long you plan to stay. This calculator compares the total cost of renting with the net cost of buying over a period you choose, after counting the equity you'd own at the end.
How to use this calculator
1. Home price: the price of the home you'd buy.
2. Down payment (%): the share you'd pay upfront.
3. Mortgage rate: the annual interest rate.
4. Mortgage term (years): the length of the loan, usually 30 in the US, or the amortization period in Canada and the UK (often 25).
5. Comparison period (years): how long you expect to stay.
6. Monthly rent: the rent for a comparable home.
7. Rent increase per year (%): expected annual rent growth.
8. Home maintenance (% of value per year): repairs and upkeep; 1% is a common rule of thumb.
Results: total rent cost, net buy cost (after equity), the difference and a note saying which costs less.
How it works
Renting: the tool adds up 12 months of rent each year, raising the rent by your increase rate at the end of each year.
Buying: it adds the down payment, every monthly mortgage payment over the comparison period and annual maintenance (percentage × home price). It then subtracts the equity you'd hold at the end: home price minus the remaining loan balance.
Net buy cost = Down payment + Mortgage payments + Maintenance − (Home price − Remaining balance)
Difference = Net buy cost − Total rent
A negative difference means buying cost less over the period.
What the model leaves out: property tax, home insurance, closing and selling costs, home price growth, and the return you could earn by investing the down payment instead. The examples show how to add them.
Worked examples
Example 1: the default scenario over 7 years
$400,000 home, 20% down, 6.5% over 30 years; rent $2,200 rising 3% a year; maintenance 1%.
Monthly mortgage payment: $2,022.62
Total paid out for buying (down payment, payments, maintenance of $28,000.00): $277,899.88
Remaining balance after 7 years: $289,331.98, so equity = $110,668.02
Net buy cost: $167,231.87
Total rent: $202,289.00
Difference: −$35,057.13, so buying looks cheaper by this measure.
Example 2: adding the costs the tool leaves out
Using the same scenario:
Closing costs of 3% plus selling costs of 6% add $36,000.00, raising the buy cost to $203,231.87 (difference $942.87: now renting is cheaper).
Home price growth of 3% a year would lift the value to $491,949.55, a gain of $91,949.55 that reduces the buy cost.
Investing the $80,000 down payment at 5% instead would have earned about $32,568.03; that forgone return adds to the cost of buying.
Net of all three: buy cost $143,850.36 against rent $202,289.00, a difference of −$58,438.65.
Over a short stay the transaction costs dominate. Over 3 years, the net buy cost is $73,349.41 before transaction costs ($109,349.41 with them) against $81,599.76 of rent.
Understanding your results
Time horizon matters most. Buying has large upfront and exit costs; the longer you stay, the more they're spread out and the more equity you build.
Early payments are mostly interest. In year one of a 30-year loan, most of each payment is interest; see the amortization calculator.
Renting has flexibility value that doesn't appear in the numbers: you can move without selling, and the landlord pays for major repairs.
The comparison is only fair if the renter invests the difference. If renting is cheaper each month but the savings get spent, the buyer may still end up wealthier.
Country notes
United States: mortgage interest and property tax are deductible only if you itemize, which fewer households do since the standard deduction ($32,200 for married couples filing jointly in 2026) rose. Gains on a main home up to $250,000 ($500,000 for couples) are excluded from tax if you meet the ownership and use tests. Use the closing cost calculator for upfront costs.
Canada: buyers pay land transfer tax (plus a municipal tax in Toronto), and CMHC insurance if the down payment is under 20%. Gains on a principal residence are generally tax-free. First-time buyers can save in an FHSA.
United Kingdom: Stamp Duty Land Tax (England and Northern Ireland), LBTT (Scotland) or LTT (Wales) can be a large upfront cost, with relief for first-time buyers in some cases. Leasehold flats add ground rent and service charges. Mortgages are usually fixed for 2 to 5 years, so the rate will change during a long comparison.
Tips and common mistakes
Comparing rent with the mortgage payment alone. Owners also pay maintenance, tax, insurance and transaction costs.
Using the comparison period as the mortgage term. A 7-year stay doesn't mean a 7-year loan.
Assuming prices always rise. Try a 0% or negative growth scenario.
Ignoring the down payment's opportunity cost.
Frequently asked questions
How long do I need to stay for buying to make sense?
Often 5 years or more, but it depends on prices, rates and transaction costs. Run the calculator with several comparison periods.
Is rent money wasted?
No. Rent buys housing and flexibility, just as mortgage interest, tax and maintenance do for owners. Only the principal repayment builds equity.
What maintenance percentage should I use?
1% of the home's value a year is a common starting point; older homes may need more.
Does the calculator include home appreciation?
Not yet. Example 2 shows how to account for it by hand.
Why does the net buy cost subtract equity?
Because at the end of the period you'd own that share of the home, which you could sell.
Should I include the price-to-rent ratio?
It's a useful screen: home price ÷ annual rent. Here 400,000 ÷ $26,400.00 ≈ 15. Higher ratios generally favour renting.
