H1: Calorie Calculator
This calculator estimates how many calories (energy) your body uses in a day. It first works out the energy you burn at rest, then scales that up for your activity level. The result is a starting estimate for maintaining your weight, which you can adjust for a gradual loss or gain. Individual needs vary, so treat the number as a starting point and adjust it based on how your weight actually changes over several weeks.
How to use this calculator
1. Choose metric or imperial, and kcal or kJ for the result.
2. Enter age, sex, height and weight. The default equations were developed for adults, so the calculator is intended for ages 18 and over.
3. Choose an activity level:
Sedentary: little or no exercise
Light: exercise 1–3 days a week
Moderate: 3–5 days a week
Very active: 6–7 days a week
Extra active: physical job plus training
4. Optional: a body-fat percentage, to use the Katch-McArdle equation instead.
5. Choose a goal: maintain, lose or gain.
The formula
Step 1: resting energy (BMR) with Mifflin-St Jeor (1990):
Men:   BMR = 10 × weight(kg) + 6.25 × height(cm) − 5 × age(y) + 5
Women: BMR = 10 × weight(kg) + 6.25 × height(cm) − 5 × age(y) − 161
Step 2: total daily energy expenditure (TDEE):
TDEE = BMR × activity factor
Commonly used factors are 1.2 (sedentary), 1.375 (light), 1.55 (moderate), 1.725 (very active) and 1.9 (extra active).
Alternatives offered:
Revised Harris-Benedict (Roza & Shizgal, 1984):
men 88.362 + 13.397w + 4.799h − 5.677a
women 447.593 + 9.247w + 3.098h − 4.330a
Katch-McArdle: BMR = 370 + 21.6 × lean body mass (kg). This needs your body-fat percentage.
Units: 1 kcal = 4.184 kJ.
Worked examples
Example 1: woman, 35, 165 cm, 65 kg, moderately active.
BMR = 10 × 65 + 6.25 × 165 − 5 × 35 − 161 = 650 + 1,031.25 − 175 − 161 = 1,345.25 kcal/day
TDEE = 1,345.25 × 1.55 = 2,085 kcal/day (about 8,724 kJ)

Example 2: a modest calorie deficit. Aiming to eat about 500 kcal a day below the 2,085 maintenance estimate gives about 1,585 kcal/day. Research behind the NIH's Body Weight Planner (Hall et al., 2011) shows that weight loss slows over time as the body adapts. A fixed rule such as "3,500 kcal per pound" therefore overestimates long-term loss. The calculator links to that planner if you want a time-based projection.
Example 3: man, 45, 6 ft, 200 lb, light activity. 200 lb = 90.7 kg and 6 ft = 182.9 cm. BMR = 1,830 kcal/day; × 1.375 = 2,517 kcal/day to maintain weight.
Understanding your results
Maintenance calories (TDEE): an estimate of what keeps your weight stable. Track your weight for 2–4 weeks and adjust by about 100–200 kcal if it moves.
Equation choice: Mifflin-St Jeor is the default for most adults. Katch-McArdle can suit people who know their body-fat percentage, particularly if they are very lean or muscular.
Activity level is the biggest source of error. Most people overestimate their activity, so if in doubt, choose the lower level.
General reference values: the NHS says that, as a guide, an average man needs 2,500 kcal a day and an average woman 2,000 kcal. Your estimate may differ from these.
Safety: large deficits aren't appropriate for everyone. If you're pregnant, breastfeeding, under 18, have a medical condition, or have a history of eating disorders, ask a doctor or registered dietitian before changing what you eat.
Country notes
US: the Dietary Guidelines for Americans publish estimated calorie needs by age, sex and activity level.
UK: food labels show both kJ and kcal. Use the kJ toggle to compare directly.
Canada: nutrition labels use Calories (kcal). Health Canada's guidance focuses on food choices rather than calorie targets.
Tips and common mistakes
Overestimating activity. Choose the lower level if unsure.
Cutting calories too far. Large deficits can be hard to sustain and aren't suitable for everyone.
Ignoring the trend. Adjust after two to four weeks of weighing.
Counting exercise twice. Your activity level already includes it.
Frequently asked questions
How many calories should I eat a day?
It depends on your size, age, sex and activity. The calculator's maintenance figure is a personalised starting estimate. Population guides, such as the NHS's 2,000 kcal for women and 2,500 kcal for men, are averages.
What's the difference between BMR and TDEE?
BMR is the energy used at complete rest. TDEE adds the energy for daily movement and exercise. See the BMR calculator for more detail.
Which formula is most accurate?
None is exact for every individual. Mifflin-St Jeor is widely used for healthy adults. If you know your lean body mass, Katch-McArdle is an alternative. Real-world tracking beats any formula.
How quickly will I lose weight with a 500 kcal deficit?
It varies. Early loss is often faster (partly water), then slows as your body adapts and your weight drops. Use the NIH Body Weight Planner for a dynamic estimate.
Should I eat back calories burned in exercise?
If you chose an activity level that already includes your exercise, no. Adding exercise calories on top would count them twice.
Can I use this if I'm pregnant?
Energy needs change during pregnancy and breastfeeding. Ask your midwife, doctor or dietitian rather than relying on this calculator.
